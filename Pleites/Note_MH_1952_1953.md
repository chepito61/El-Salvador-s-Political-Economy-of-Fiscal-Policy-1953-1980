# El Salvador's Fiscal Landscape, 1952–1953: Evidence from the Memorias de Hacienda

**Working Note — Based on Primary Sources**

---

## Introduction

This note presents a preliminary synthesis of the economic and fiscal situation of El Salvador during the fiscal years 1952 and 1953, drawn exclusively from the *Informes Complementarios Constitucionales* (Memorias de Hacienda) published by the Ministry of Finance (*Ministerio de Hacienda*). These reports, submitted annually to the Legislative Assembly under Article 78 of the Constitution, constitute a rich primary source on the fiscal operations of the Salvadoran state during the mid-twentieth century.

The period under review corresponds to the early years of the Óscar Osorio administration (1950–1956), a modernizing military government that undertook significant reforms in public administration and fiscal management. The Memorias reveal a state in the process of building modern budgetary institutions while navigating an economy highly dependent on coffee exports.

---

## 1. Budget Modernization

A recurring theme across both Memorias is the effort to modernize the budget process. Before 1952, El Salvador's budgets were constructed on the basis of *partidas específicas de egreso* — rigid line items that allocated funds to narrowly defined expenditure categories (rent, office supplies, subscriptions, etc.). The Ministry noted that this system frequently impeded the normal operations of government agencies: some line items carried unspent balances while others, facing urgent needs, had no available funds.

In 1952, the government introduced a transitional budget structure based on *apropiaciones globales* — broad appropriation classes — as a stepping stone toward the *Presupuesto por Actividades* (activity-based budgeting). This was complemented by a *sistema de cuotas* (quota system), implemented from April 1952, which authorized spending by agency, period, and purpose, giving the Ministry of Finance real-time capacity to align public expenditure with available fiscal resources.

By 1953, El Salvador had fully adopted the *presupuesto por actividades* — a system already in use in several Latin American countries. The 1953 Memoria emphasizes the advantages of this approach: it allowed the government to know, both at the time of preparation and during execution, the cost and scale of each program, as well as whether budgeted resources were sufficient to meet planned activities. This also facilitated functional and economic classification of expenditures, a prerequisite for fiscal analysis of the kind promoted by international organizations in the postwar period.

## 2. Revenue Structure and Performance

### 2.1 Revenue Levels and Growth

The Memorias document a period of rapid revenue growth. In 1952, total revenues collected and accrued (*rentas percibidas y devengadas*) reached approximately **₡135.2 million**. By 1953, this figure rose to approximately **₡140.3 million**, a net increase of roughly ₡12.8 million or about 10.9% over the previous year.

The 1953 report provides a striking five-year perspective. Total fiscal revenues had roughly doubled between 1949 and 1953:

| Fiscal Year | Total Revenue (₡ millions) | % Increase over Prior Year |
|:-----------:|:--------------------------:|:--------------------------:|
| 1949        | 69.1                       | —                          |
| 1950        | 83.4                       | 21%                        |
| 1951        | 117.7                      | 41%                        |
| 1952        | 127.7                      | 8%                         |
| 1953        | 140.4                      | 10%                        |

The Ministry attributed this upward trend to the sustained expansion of economic activity, particularly the growth of coffee exports and related import activity, combined with the impact of tax reforms on customs and direct taxes.

### 2.2 Revenue Composition

The revenue structure was overwhelmingly dependent on indirect taxation, particularly trade taxes. In 1953, the broad category of *Impuestos* (taxes) accounted for approximately **86.7%** of total revenue. Within this category:

- **Import and export duties** (*Derechos de Importación y Exportación*) constituted approximately 82% of all tax revenue and were by far the single largest revenue source. Import duties alone represented the dominant share.
- **Customs revenue** (*Renta de Aduanas*) was the largest single revenue division but showed vulnerability to external shocks. In 1952, customs revenue declined relative to 1951, reflecting lower coffee prices, reduced import/export volumes, and the end of the exceptional 1950–51 coffee boom.
- **Direct taxes** (*Impuestos Directos*) remained a minor share of total revenue. The 1952 report notes that within the revenue items that registered increases, direct taxes accounted for about 19.7% of total increases, with the chapter on *Explotación de Empresas Oficiales* representing 39.4%.

The reports also document the distinction between *rentas percibidas* (cash-basis collections) and *rentas devengadas* (accrued revenues), noting that the difference — representing uncollected customs duties on outstanding import/export policies — amounted to about 0.5% of total estimated revenues in 1952.

## 3. Expenditure Structure

### 3.1 Total Expenditure

In 1952, total expenditures (gastos y compromisos) charged to the General Fund reached approximately **₡112.1 million**. The expenditure breakdown by economic classification was:

| Category                                    | Amount (₡)      | % of Total |
|:--------------------------------------------|:---------------:|:----------:|
| Servicios Personales (Personnel)            | 53,965,968      | 48.2%      |
| Servicios no Personales                     | 6,162,400       | 5.5%       |
| Materiales, Artículos y Repuestos           | 11,353,085      | 10.1%      |
| Equipo (Equipment)                          | 3,310,886       | 3.0%       |
| Propiedad (Property)                        | 2,129,393       | 1.9%       |
| Gratificaciones, Subs., Donativos y Rec.    | 23,588,747      | 21.1%      |
| Amortización de la Deuda Pública e Int.     | 5,168,456       | 4.6%       |
| Otros Egresos                               | 1,715,034       | 1.5%       |
| **Total**                                   | **112,074,041** | **100%**   |

The debt service line — *Amortización de la Deuda Pública e Intereses* — represented **4.6%** of total expenditure in 1952, amounting to roughly **₡5.2 million**. This is a combined figure for principal amortization and interest payments.

By 1953, total authorized allocations (*asignaciones autorizadas*) rose to **₡144.2 million**, including an additional capital credit of ₡2.67 million authorized by Decreto Legislativo. Of these, ₡124.1 million were effectively utilized. The expenditure by economic class in 1953 was:

| Propiedad                                   | 6,881,601       | 5.6%       |
| Subsidios, Subvenciones y Donativos         | 26,354,074      | 21.2%      |

| Otros Egresos                               | 4,799,887       | 3.9%       |
| **Total**                                   | **124,053,563** | **100%**   |

Debt service in 1953 remained at a similar level: approximately **₡5.3 million**, or **4.3%** of total expenditure.

Public works and defense together absorbed over 28% of total spending, reflecting the government's dual priorities of infrastructure development and military consolidation.

## 4. Public Debt

### 4.1 Debt Service

As noted above, the combined line for amortization of public debt and interest payments was approximately ₡5.2 million in 1952 and ₡5.3 million in 1953. The Memorias do not fully disaggregate amortization from interest in the main expenditure tables, but these figures establish that debt service consumed roughly **4–5%** of total government expenditure during this period — a modest burden.

### 4.2 Outstanding Obligations and Borrowing

The 1953 Memoria provides detail on the state's borrowing. It notes that total bonds and letters (*bonos y letras*) held by the *Agente Fiscal del Estado* (the Central Reserve Bank) as of December 31, 1953, amounted to **₡24,205,457.33**. This included the outstanding balance of the *Empréstito Nacional de 1922*, from which amortization and interest paid during the year totaled approximately **₡668,378** (amortization) and an undisclosed interest amount.

The balance sheet (*Balance General de Situación*) as of December 31, 1953, shows:

- **Total Assets (Fondo General):** ₡58,853,272.73 (circulating) + ₡6,968,971.38 (permanent) = ₡65,822,244.11
- **Total Liabilities:** ₡53,386,783.13 (circulating) + ₡8,306,062.89 (provisions and reserves)
- **Superávit Financiero:** ₡4,061,831.18 (carried from 1952) adjusted to approximately ₡1,257,000 after the year's operations.

The report mentions several special funds with associated debt obligations, including the *Carretera Panamericana* (Pan-American Highway), the *Empréstito Interno de Aprovisionamiento*, and the *Fondo del Muelle de Acajutla* (Acajutla Port), each carrying their own balance positions.

## 5. Economic Context

The Memorias, while primarily fiscal documents, reveal important elements of the broader economic context:

- **Coffee dependence:** The revenue structure's heavy reliance on import and export duties made the fiscal position highly sensitive to coffee price cycles. The 1952 report explicitly attributes the decline in customs revenue to lower coffee prices and reduced trade volumes following the exceptional boom of 1950–51.
- **Modernization agenda:** The government was investing heavily in infrastructure (public works as the largest spending category in 1953) and administrative reform (budget modernization, new accounting systems). The creation of dedicated funds for the Pan-American Highway, the Acajutla port, and the *Valle de La Esperanza* rehabilitation and development project reflects a developmentalist orientation.
- **Fiscal conservatism:** Despite the modernizing thrust, the government maintained a relatively conservative fiscal posture. Debt service was modest (4–5% of expenditure), and the reports repeatedly emphasize the importance of aligning expenditure with available resources through the quota system. The government ran fiscal surpluses in both years.
- **Institutional building:** The transition to activity-based budgeting, the implementation of new government accounting standards (*contabilidad gubernamental*), and the effort to produce detailed financial statements all reflect a state actively building the institutional infrastructure for modern fiscal management.

---

## Preliminary Observations

1. **Revenue buoyancy masked structural vulnerability.** The doubling of revenues between 1949 and 1953 reflected favorable external conditions (the Korean War-era commodity boom) more than domestic tax effort. The heavy reliance on trade taxes — particularly coffee-linked customs duties — left the fisc highly exposed to terms-of-trade shocks.

2. **Expenditure priorities reveal the political economy.** The concentration of spending on public works and defense, with relatively modest allocations to health and social services, is consistent with the priorities of a modernizing military government focused on infrastructure-led development.

3. **Debt was not yet a constraint.** With service absorbing only 4–5% of expenditure and the bulk of obligations consisting of legacy instruments (the 1922 National Loan) and project-specific funds, the government had significant fiscal space for borrowing — space that would be tested in subsequent years as development ambitions grew.

4. **Institutional modernization preceded policy ambition.** The investment in budget reform and financial management systems during 1952–53 created the technical capacity that would later support more ambitious fiscal programs in the second half of the decade.
