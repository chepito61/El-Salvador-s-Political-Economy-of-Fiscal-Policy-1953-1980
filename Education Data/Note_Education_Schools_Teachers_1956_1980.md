# El Salvador: Schools, Teachers and Enrolment, 1956–1980

**Working Note — A provisional series from published sources (November 2026)**

> **About this note.** This note builds a first series of schools, teachers and enrolment to support hypothesis H2: whether the wage bill grew through more teachers or higher pay per teacher. It complements the fiscal series in the Memorias de Hacienda, which give the education wage bill but not the number of posts. All figures come from published secondary sources, listed at the end. Each has its own definitions, and some conflict; conflicts are flagged. The data file is *Education Data/schools_teachers_enrollment_1956_1980.csv* in the repository (100 data points, each with its source and a confidence flag).

**Reading.**

- **Primary enrolment grew about 4–5% a year through the Rivera and Sánchez Hernández years,** faster than the population (3.2% a year).
- **Secondary enrolment jumped after the 1968 reform,** which created the diversified *bachillerato*.
- **University enrolment almost quadrupled in seven years** (1967–74). That growth is the backdrop to the University's rising claims on the budget, and to the conflicts of 1968–72.

**The number of schools was almost flat (about 3,000–3,250) while enrolment grew by half,** so crowding rose. In the 1970s about 64% of primary schools were rural, and about 70% of rural schools did not go beyond grade four (Library of Congress).

| Ministry of Education employees | about 17,000 | 1960s | UES thesis (Orellana) |

**Rough estimate (to be replaced by Diario Oficial data).** At about 40 pupils per teacher, primary enrolment implies roughly **12,000 primary teachers in 1967** and **15,000 in 1973**.

As a check against the fiscal data: in 1966 primary-education personal services cost ₡31.1M (Memoria de Hacienda 1966). Spread over about 12,000 teachers, that is roughly ₡2,600 a year, or ₡215 a month. This is plausible for an average that includes rural and unqualified teachers, below the ₡400 reported for the early 1970s. **This is an order of magnitude only.**

## 2. What it means for the hypotheses

1. **A teacher surplus and a hiring bottleneck (H2).** By 1967 the private teacher-training schools were producing about four times as many teachers as the Ministry could hire. Thousands of qualified teachers were unemployed while the share of unqualified teachers in the classroom rose. This combination of surplus, low pay and the 1968 reform's closure of the teacher-training schools is a plausible material base for the founding of ANDES 21 de Junio (1965) and the national teachers' strikes of 1967–68 and 1971.
2. **Posts versus pay.** The Memorias show the primary wage bill rising 7.0% (1964), 2.6% (1965) and about 7.5% (1966, unit total). Without post counts we cannot yet say whether this was more teachers or higher salaries per teacher. The fiscal record alone is consistent with either.
3. **The University's share.** University enrolment grew far faster than primary enrolment. According to the World Bank (1973), cost per student was about US$40 in primary and US$800 in higher education. Budget growth for the University is therefore not by itself evidence of political favour; it partly reflects enrolment.
