# Reading Note: Zamora, *Siglo XX. Sociedad y Estado* (2019)

**Working note — 21 November 2026.** Background for the narrative briefing for the research assistants. Context only: figures in our notes still come from the Memorias de Hacienda.

**Reference.** Zamora, Rubén (2019). *Siglo XX. Sociedad y Estado* (Colección Bicentenario, 3). San Salvador: Instituto Nacional de Formación Docente (INFOD). 86 pp. File: *Historical Background/Zamora_2019_Siglo_XX_Sociedad_y_Estado.pdf*.

**What it is.** A short interpretive essay on the Salvadoran State in the twentieth century, written for public-school teachers. It reads the State on three levels: economy and politics, the State apparatus, and the actors. Chapter III (pp. 41–61) covers our period, 1948–1979, which Zamora calls "organic militarism" and "the militarist, social and developmentalist model". The spending figures are his own calculations from Knut Walter (2014), *Las políticas culturales del Estado salvadoreño, 1900–2012*.

---

## 1. Where it supports our frame

**Developmentalism and repression as one model.** Zamora's 1948 regime rests on three constants: verbal constitutionalism, developmentalism, and the official party as the link to citizens (p. 45). Already under Martínez the regime "combined developmentalism with repressive violence" (p. 40). This is the development and security pillars, stated by a historian.

**The division of labour with the elite.** From Martínez on, the high command governs and business runs the economy. The military consults business on State policy, and business keeps a veto (p. 42). This is our coalition pillar and the tax bargain (Track A). Zamora also notes that the elite kept the press, radio and television.

**The State built institutions and handed them to private control.** The Banco Hipotecario (1934) was owned 40% by the Asociación Cafetalera and 20% by the Asociación Ganadera. The Banco Central de Reserva (1934) was also founded as a private company (*sociedad anónima*). "The State institutionalizes, but control is handed to private hands", and later military governments repeated the scheme (pp. 35–36). This matters for H4: the Central Bank that financed the Treasury from 1969 was set up under this bargain.

**Modernization from above.** Under Osorio, ministries grew from four (grouping fourteen portfolios) to ten, and autonomous institutions were created: IVU, IRA, ICR, ISSS, CEPA and CEL. Tax collection was modernized (p. 48). The industrial promotion law (30 May 1952), ODECA (1951) and the Common Market treaty (December 1960) followed one strategy: the domestic market was too small, so industry needed the isthmus (p. 52).

**Fiscal autonomy from coffee was partial.** Zamora argues that public spending gradually became less dependent on coffee revenue, thanks to cotton, industry and better tax collection. He qualifies this: the effect was on tax collection, not on the economy, where coffee still mattered (p. 48). Our series agrees for the 1960s and disagrees for the 1970s, when coffee returned to a quarter of revenue.

**The Cold War and the national security doctrine.** "Salvadoran militarism is born and dies with the Cold War." The Armed Forces adopted Cold War anti-communism as their ideological frame and main task. In exchange, Washington treated the army as the guarantor of containment, through a direct relationship with the high command (p. 44).

**Crises every twelve years.** Coups came in 1931, 1944, 1948, 1960, 1972 (attempted) and 1979. Each one coincided with three things: an economic crisis, usually a fall in coffee prices; rising urban mobilization; and a change in US policy toward the region (p. 45). This is a testable claim for our coffee-cycle series.

**Rivera took the security side of the Alliance for Progress.** Rivera focused on the anti-communist security proposals of the Alliance more than on its social goals (p. 53). Under him ORDEN (1966) and ANSESAL were created, both founded by General Medrano (p. 54). In the countryside, control ran through the National Guard, the Treasury Police and the *patrullas cantonales* of ex-conscripts (p. 55). Much of this was cheap or off the ordinary budget, which fits our reading that the security pillar was larger than the ministry totals show.

**The new middle groups.** Under the PCN, urbanization, the State bureaucracy and industry expanded the middle sectors and the urban working class (pp. 54–55). These groups produced the opposition: the PDC's 14, 15 and 19 seats (1964, 1966, 1968), FUSS (14 unions in 1965, 40 by 1967), FECCAS, and the 1968 teachers' strike, which followed the 1967 ACERO strike (p. 56).

**1976: the limit of the State's autonomy.** The ISTA project was withdrawn under pressure from the unified business sector and the landowners' paramilitary FARO. This showed "the government's weakness and lack of autonomy from the holders of economic power" (p. 57). This supports Track D.

## 2. Spending figures (Walter's data, Zamora's calculations)

| Item | Zamora (quinquennial shares of spending) |
|:--|:--|
| Education ("Cultura Popular") | 16.37% (first half of the 1950s), 14.92% (second half); 24% and 25% in the next two decades; about 10% before 1950 |
| Public works | Over 15% in the 1950s; 10% in the 1960s; 11.52% and 14.64% in the two halves of the 1970s |
| Defence plus public security | 14.47% and 13.41% in the 1950s; 8.44% and 10.29% thereafter (his text is ambiguous about which quinquennia) |
| Total spending | Budget of ₡76.8M in 1950, ₡162.5M in 1955; average ₡167.2M in 1955–59 |
| Presidency | ₡0.14M to ₡0.21M in the 1930s; peak ₡8.0M in 1958; ₡4–5M in the 1960s; ₡7.4M in 1970 to ₡20.3M in 1974 |
| War ministry under Martínez | 17.5% (1930–34) and 18.5% (1937–41); up to 19%, the largest item |
| Public debt service | Over 30% of spending in 1875–1911; 22.8% (1930–34) and 8.6–8.8% (1937–41) after the renegotiations |

**Against our series.** The direction agrees with our verified series: security fell from 13–16% in the 1950s, and education rose to a quarter of spending. The levels differ, because Walter's basis is not ours (budget or executed spending, and his ministry groupings). His 10.29% for security in the 1970s is above our 6.5% for 1976, and his education shares are a little below our 27% peak in 1971. **The Presidency series is useful:** its rise to ₡20.3M in 1974 matches the unspecified lump sums of ₡19.4M we found in the Presidency in 1977. The content of those sums remains an open question for the Diario Oficial.

The book's annex tables cover only 1897–1927, not our period.

## 3. Use in the briefing

- **Historical background:** the 1948 regime, the 1950 Constitution, Osorio's institutions, 1960, Rivera and the Alliance, the opposition's rise, 1972, 1976, 1977 and October 1979.
- **The frame:** the three constants of the regime, the division of labour with the elite, and the Cold War doctrine.
- **A check on our figures:** Walter's long series for education, security and the Presidency.
- **A source to obtain:** Walter (2014), which has the full annual series behind these shares. It could fill our gaps before 1952 and in 1957–59.

**Caution.** Zamora was a leader of the opposition FDR during the war, so he writes partly as a participant. The essay has few citations for the 1948–1979 period, and dates should be checked before use. For example, his 56-day teachers' strike follows the 1967 ACERO strike, so it is the 1968 strike, not the 53-day strike of 1971 in our notes.
