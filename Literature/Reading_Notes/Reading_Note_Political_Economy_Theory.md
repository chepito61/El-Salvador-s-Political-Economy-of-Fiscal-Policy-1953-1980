This note reviews seven papers on the political economy of institutions, regimes and the state, read against the research question: whether the fiscal and monetary management of a small, open, agro-exporting economy contributed to the political polarization that led to the civil war. Together they give the project a formal frame for the bargain among the coffee elite, the military and the groups created by modernization. One of them, Nugent and Robinson (2010), is about El Salvador directly, and Acemoglu, Ticchi and Vindigni (2010) use El Salvador as an example.

| Paper | What it adds |
|:--|:--|
| **Acemoglu and Robinson (2000)**, "Why Did the West Extend the Franchise?" | Concessions within an unchanged regime are not credible; only institutional change commits the elite to future redistribution |
| **Acemoglu and Robinson (2001)**, "A Theory of Political Transitions" | Revolts and coups are more likely in recessions; unequal societies oscillate between regimes and have volatile fiscal policy |
| **Acemoglu and Robinson (2008)**, "Persistence of Power, Elites, and Institutions" | Elites offset changes in formal (de jure) power with de facto power: wealth, capture, paramilitaries |
| **Acemoglu, Ticchi and Vindigni (2010)**, "A Theory of Military Dictatorships" | An elite that builds an army for repression faces an agency problem; the army may take power for itself. El Salvador cited |

# 1. The papers

## 1.1 Acemoglu and Robinson (2000), "Why Did the West Extend the Franchise?"

**Reference.** Acemoglu, Daron, and James A. Robinson (2000). "Why Did the West Extend the Franchise? Democracy, Inequality, and Growth in Historical Perspective." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 115(4): 1167–1199.

**Argument.** Nineteenth-century elites extended the vote to prevent unrest and revolution. They could not simply redistribute within the existing regime, because "current transfers do not ensure future transfers": once the threat passes, the elite can withdraw them. Extending the franchise changes who decides in the future, so it commits the elite to redistribution (p. 1167). Democratization in Britain was followed by higher and more progressive taxation and by mass education.

**For our question.** This is the most useful single idea in the set. The Salvadoran regime responded to pressure with **temporary concessions that it could withdraw**: the 1961 income-tax reform, diluted within months; the 1976 agrarian transformation, withdrawn under pressure in October; budget-financed expansions of education and public works that depended on coffee money. It did not offer the institutional change that would have made the concessions credible: the elections of 1972 and 1977 were stolen. In the model, when temporary concessions are not credible and institutional change is refused, the threat of revolution can only be met by repression. The fiscal record measures the concessions: development spending as temporary redistribution, and a tax system that never changed who paid.

## 1.2 Acemoglu and Robinson (2001), "A Theory of Political Transitions"

**Reference.** Acemoglu, Daron, and James A. Robinson (2001). "A Theory of Political Transitions." *American Economic Review* 91(4): 938–963. (The repository file is labelled 2010.)

**Argument.** A rich elite controls a nondemocratic regime. The poor can threaten revolution, "especially when the opportunity cost is low, for example, during recessions" (p. 938). The threat may force democratization, but democracy may not consolidate, because it redistributes and so gives the elite an incentive to mount a coup. "Highly unequal societies are less likely to consolidate democracy, and may end up oscillating between regimes and suffer substantial fiscal volatility." Regime changes, both transitions and coups, are more likely in recessions (p. 939, citing Haggard and Kaufman, Gasiorowski and Przeworski).

**For our question.** It links the coffee cycle to political crises through a precise mechanism: a bust lowers the cost of revolt. It can be tested with our boom and bust classification against the crises of 1960 (after the coffee fall of 1958–59), 1972 (after the 1971 bust) and 1979 (after the 1978 bust). Zamora makes the same link between falling coffee prices and coups. The prediction of fiscal volatility in unequal societies matches our finding that spending followed the coffee cycle.

## 1.3 Acemoglu and Robinson (2008), "Persistence of Power, Elites, and Institutions"

**Reference.** Acemoglu, Daron, and James A. Robinson (2008). "Persistence of Power, Elites, and Institutions." *American Economic Review* 98(1): 267–293.

**Argument.** Formal (de jure) political power is allocated by institutions; de facto power comes from wealth, weapons and organization. When institutions change, elites invest in de facto power, "bribery, the capture of political parties, or use of paramilitaries", to offset the change (p. 268). Economic institutions can therefore persist through political reforms, including "captured democracy". Labour-repressive institutions are more likely in agricultural societies.

**For our question.** It explains why reforms announced by the State did not change outcomes: the landowners' paramilitary FARO against the 1976 project, the business-financed death squads of the late 1970s, and the elite's veto through the press and the business associations (Zamora). It also frames the failure of the October 1979 Junta's reforms.

## 1.4 Acemoglu, Ticchi and Vindigni (2010), "A Theory of Military Dictatorships"

**Reference.** Acemoglu, Daron, Davide Ticchi and Andrea Vindigni (2010). "A Theory of Military Dictatorships." *American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics* 2(1): 1–42.

**Argument.** An elite facing redistributive pressure may build an army to repress. But the army is not automatically its agent: the elite must pay it enough (an "efficiency wage") or risk the army turning against it. With effective repression and high inequality, the elite build a large army; under some conditions the army acts in its own interest and takes power (pp. 3–4). Very unequal nondemocracies spend little on public goods and much on the military, and "nondemocracies should typically have more military spending than democracies" (pp. 4, 6).

**El Salvador in the paper.** The authors cite the Salvadoran regime of the 1950s under Osorio as a military dictatorship and contrast Central America with Costa Rica, which disbanded its army in 1949: "highly repressive military dictatorships were established in the 1950s in Guatemala and El Salvador … the militaries, which were initially created by the elite for repression of the lower strata in these highly polarized societies, ultimately became strong enough to seize power and establish their own dictatorships" (p. 5).

**For our question: a prediction our data contradict.** On spending, the model predicts that El Salvador's military regime would spend little on public goods and much on the military. Our series shows the opposite: development rose to about half of spending, and security fell from 13–16% in the 1950s to 6.5% in 1976. Three readings reconcile the two, and each can be tested:

1. **The army had its own project.** The model allows the army to act in its own interest. Ching's reformist *tandas* and the regime's modernization from above fit this: the military used the budget to build a developmental state, not only to repress for the elite.
2. **Repression was cheap and off the budget.** Paramilitary networks (ORDEN, the *patrullas cantonales*) and extraordinary budgets carried much of the security effort (see 1.5).
3. **The turn came with scarcity.** Security spending doubled only in 1978–79, when the coffee bust and the threat of revolution came together, as the model would predict.

## 1.5 Acemoglu, Robinson and Santos (2013), "The Monopoly of Violence"

**Reference.** Acemoglu, Daron, James A. Robinson and Rafael J. Santos (2013). "The Monopoly of Violence: Evidence from Colombia." *Journal of the European Economic Association* 11(S1): 5–44.

**Argument.** Many states lack the monopoly of violence. Paramilitaries persist because they can influence elections, and the politicians they help have little incentive to eliminate them. Evidence from Colombia, 1991–2006, including a "quid pro quo" visible in congressional votes.

**For our question.** ORDEN, which recruited up to 100,000 rural people (Ching), was state-sponsored rather than independent, but the logic is related: rural paramilitary networks policed the countryside and delivered the rural vote to the official party, at little cost to the budget. This is the mechanism behind reading 2 above, and a reason why the security pillar understates the regime's coercive effort.

## 1.6 Nugent and Robinson (2010), "Are Factor Endowments Fate?"

**Reference.** Nugent, Jeffrey B., and James A. Robinson (2010). "Are Factor Endowments Fate?" *Revista de Historia Económica / Journal of Iberian and Latin American Economic History* 28(1): 45–82.

**Argument.** Colombia, Costa Rica, El Salvador and Guatemala had similar climates, endowments, technology and the same export crop. Colombia and Costa Rica developed coffee through smallholders; El Salvador and Guatemala through large plantations. The difference came from nineteenth-century land law: Costa Rica and Colombia passed laws protecting smallholders, while in El Salvador and Guatemala "the onset of the coffee boom induced a mass land grab by powerful political elites … Alongside such land grabs came extreme labor repression" (p. 49). Two causes: politics in El Salvador and Guatemala was more militarized, and their elites were landowners rather than merchants and financiers (pp. 49–50).

**Education.** "There is a natural connection between the type of coercion that characterized the labor market in El Salvador and Guatemala and the refusal of the state to invest in education … in a labor market based on coercion, workers were unlikely to believe that they would receive any return from exerting effort. In consequence, it is unlikely that there would be much payoff to landowning elites for investing in schools" (p. 51). The organization of the coffee economy shaped "the incentives of political elites to invest resources in education" (p. 78).

**For our question.** This is the historical base of your frame: the coffee economy needed little education, and its elite had no interest in providing it. The military regime's expansion of education after 1948 broke with the coffee economy's logic. That break is where the development pillar met the interests of the coffee elite, and where the educated groups it produced met an economy still organized around coffee.

## 1.7 Besley and Persson (2009), "The Origins of State Capacity"

**Reference.** Besley, Timothy, and Torsten Persson (2009). "The Origins of State Capacity: Property Rights, Taxation, and Politics." *American Economic Review* 99(4): 1218–1244.

**Argument.** The taxes and regulations a state can apply are constrained by past investments in fiscal and legal capacity, which are complements. Governments invest in capacity when there are common-interest public goods, above all external war, and when politics is stable and inclusive. A more polarized or less representative system, where the groups in and out of power weigh each other's welfare less, invests less. Income taxes were born of war: Britain in 1798, the United States in 1861 (p. 1219).

**For our question.** El Salvador's fiscal capacity stayed narrow: revenue came from the border, corporate income tax never exceeded 7% of revenue, and the 1961 reform was diluted. The 1969 war was the kind of common threat that built income taxes elsewhere, but it was financed with bonds, Central Bank credit and a one-off emergency tax, and left the tax base unchanged. The model's explanation, a narrow and unrepresentative regime, fits Track A (the tax bargain).

# 2. What the set adds to the project

**A formal frame for the bargain.** The papers give names and mechanisms to the actors already in our notes: an elite whose power rested on land and coffee (Nugent and Robinson); a military built for repression that developed its own project (Acemoglu, Ticchi and Vindigni); citizens whose threat rose in recessions (Acemoglu and Robinson, 2001); and de facto power that undid formal reforms (Acemoglu and Robinson, 2008).

**Four hypotheses to test with the fiscal record.**

1. **Concessions were temporary, not committed.** Development spending and announced reforms were reversible and coffee-dependent, and the electoral route was closed. *Test:* trace each concession (education, wages, land, taxes) through the following bust.
2. **Busts raised the threat.** *Test:* line up our boom and bust years with strikes, protests and coups, 1952–1979.
3. **Repression was cheap until it was not.** *Test:* the security pillar against off-budget and paramilitary security, and its jump in 1978–79.
4. **Polarization kept fiscal capacity narrow.** *Test:* the share of direct taxes and the fate of tax reforms, 1950–1979, including after the 1969 war.

**One prediction our data contradict.** Acemoglu, Ticchi and Vindigni predict that a very unequal military regime spends little on public goods. El Salvador's regime spent heavily on development until 1978. Explaining this contradiction, through the military's own modernizing project and the cheapness of outsourced repression, is a contribution the fiscal record can make to this literature.
