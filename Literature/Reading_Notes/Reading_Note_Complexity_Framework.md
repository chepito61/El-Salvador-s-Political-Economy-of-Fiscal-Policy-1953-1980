This note reviews four papers from the growth-diagnostics and economic-complexity framework developed by Ricardo Hausmann and his co-authors. They follow up a result in Acevedo (2003): in El Salvador, schooling did not raise productivity. Each paper is read against the research question and against the frame of this project, in which the regime expanded education for a modernizing economy that could not absorb the educated groups it created.

# 1. Hausmann and Rodrik (2005), "Self-Discovery in a Development Strategy for El Salvador"

**Reference.** Hausmann, Ricardo, and Dani Rodrik (2005). "Self-Discovery in a Development Strategy for El Salvador." *Economía* 6(1): 43–101, with comments by José Miguel Benavente and Francisco Rodríguez. The paper draws on a report commissioned by FUSADES (2003) before the 2004 elections.

**The question.** El Salvador was "a star reformer" after the war but "not a star performer": real income per capita stagnated after 1997 at about the level of the late 1970s. Income per capita in 1999 was still 4.4% below 1978 (p. 63).

**The method: growth diagnostics.** Growth depends on the return to investment, the appropriability of that return, and the cost of finance. Whatever constraint is binding should show up in its price: a scarce factor earns a high return (pp. 47–51).

**Education is not the binding constraint (pp. 51–57).** If education were scarce, the returns to schooling would be high. They are not:

- Returns to schooling in El Salvador are low by Latin American standards, lower than in Brazil, Chile or Thailand.
- They **fell** between 1992 and 2002 for primary and secondary education, while enrolment grew. "Something else caused the slow growth, and the decline in the demand for skills brought down the returns to education."
- The authors' conclusion: "If growth is being constrained by other factors, more education is likely to lead mainly to lower returns to human capital rather than to higher incomes, as has been the case" (p. 57).

**The binding constraint: self-discovery.** Investment is low because entrepreneurs have few profitable new ideas. A pioneer bears the full cost of failure, while imitators share the gains of success (an information externality). New activities need complementary investments by others (a coordination externality). Firms underinvest in general training because workers leave (a training externality) (pp. 63–76). El Salvador recorded 46 new export "discoveries" in 1997–2002, against 106 in Guatemala and 59 in Honduras.

**The history it tells (pp. 66–73).**

- Between 1970 and 1978 the economy grew 48% (5.0% a year), on coffee, cotton and sugar and on manufactures sold mainly to the Common Market. The model "became unviable" with the fall in agricultural terms of trade and the collapse of the Common Market in the 1980s; the agrarian reform and the cotton pests made it worse.
- Investment was 21% of GDP in 1974–78, against 16% in 1996–2000.
- Export receipts from coffee, cotton and sugar in 1996–2002 fell short of 1974–78 by US$613 million a year, about 5% of GDP. The fall in coffee prices alone cost about 3.2% of GDP a year.
- **Import substitution was industrial policy, and it worked as such:** "protection of the domestic market (and other neighboring markets) also drove diversification and industrialization in the 1960s and 1970s. Coffee profits were invested in industry largely because high trade barriers in the region created profitable import-substituting opportunities" (pp. 72–73). Their example is the shoe company UDOC, founded in the 1950s with family money from coffee and banking, protected from imports and rescued by the family bank in its early years.
- The real exchange rate appreciated steadily from the 1970s.

**Rodríguez's comment.** The framework assumes policy relaxes one constraint at a time; relaxing several partly can do better. He also stresses that the politics of implementation must be modelled.

# 2. Hidalgo and Hausmann (2009), "The Building Blocks of Economic Complexity"

**Reference.** Hidalgo, César A., and Ricardo Hausmann (2009). "The Building Blocks of Economic Complexity." *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 106(26): 10570–10575.

**The idea.** Products are made from non-tradable "capabilities": skills, institutions, infrastructure, regulation. A country can make a product only if it has all the capabilities the product needs, as a child can build a Lego model only with the right pieces. The capabilities cannot be observed, but they can be inferred from which countries export which products.

**The measures.** Two numbers summarize the country–product network: a country's *diversity* (how many products it exports with comparative advantage) and a product's *ubiquity* (how many countries export it). The "Method of Reflections" combines them iteratively. Complex economies make many products, including products few others can make.

**The results.**

- Complexity is correlated with income, and **countries whose income is low relative to their complexity grow faster afterwards** (1985–2005): income converges to the level the productive structure can support.
- The measures are strongly correlated with the **diversity of the labour inputs** used in production. Complexity is, in part, a measure of the variety of skills an economy uses.

**Relevance.** This gives the demand side of the education story. The kinds of skills an economy can use depend on what it produces. A coffee economy needs few kinds of skill; the products of the Common Market years needed more, but not many more.

# 3. Hidalgo, Klinger, Barabási and Hausmann (2007), "The Product Space"

**Reference.** Hidalgo, C. A., B. Klinger, A.-L. Barabási and R. Hausmann (2007). "The Product Space Conditions the Development of Nations." *Science* 317(5837): 482–487.

**The idea.** Two products are "close" if countries that export one tend to export the other, because they use similar capabilities. Mapping all 775 SITC-4 products this way gives the "product space".

**The results.**

- The product space has a **core and a periphery.** Metal products, machinery and chemicals form the dense core. **Tropical agriculture, fishing, cereals, garments and textiles sit in the sparse periphery.**
- Countries move to products near those they already export. The probability of acquiring a product within five years is almost nil if the nearest existing export is distant, and about 15% if it is close.
- Latin America is in the periphery, in mining, agriculture and garments. Most poor countries can reach the core only by "jumping" distances that are rarely crossed.

**Relevance.** Coffee sits far out in the periphery. A coffee windfall, however large, does not by itself move an economy toward the core. This explains why diversification in El Salvador needed policy, as Hausmann and Rodrik note for the Common Market years, and why it went mainly into textiles, shoes, food processing and pharmaceuticals, products close to what the country already did.

# 4. Hausmann, Hwang and Rodrik (2007), "What You Export Matters"

**Reference.** Hausmann, Ricardo, Jason Hwang and Dani Rodrik (2007). "What You Export Matters." *Journal of Economic Growth* 12(1): 1–25.

**The idea.** When discovering what a country can produce at low cost ("cost discovery") creates spillovers, the mix of goods a country produces is partly indeterminate, and it matters for growth.

**The measures.** PRODY is the income level associated with a product: the weighted average income of the countries that export it. EXPY is the export-weighted average PRODY of a country's basket, "the income level of a country's exports".

**The results.** EXPY is correlated with income per capita, but some countries have export baskets far richer than their income (China is the leading example). **EXPY is a strong and robust predictor of subsequent growth,** including with instrumental variables and country fixed effects.

**Data.** Two datasets: HS 6-digit trade data for 1992–2003, and SITC 4-digit data for **1962–2000** (Feenstra et al., 2005), covering 97 countries. The second covers our 1960s and 1970s.

# 5. What the framework adds to our question

**It explains the contradiction in economic terms.** Our frame says the regime expanded education for a modernizing economy that could not absorb the educated groups it created. The framework supplies the mechanism:

1. The kinds of skill an economy can use depend on what it produces (Hidalgo and Hausmann).
2. A coffee economy is in the sparse periphery of the product space, and moves only to nearby products (Hidalgo et al.).
3. If the productive structure does not become more complex, more education lowers the returns to schooling rather than raising incomes (Hausmann and Rodrik, for 1992–2002; Acevedo, for 1962–2000).

In the 1960s and 1970s, the regime's education expansion (education reached a quarter of spending and 27% in 1971) ran ahead of the economy's capacity to employ educated workers: industry grew 8% a year but created jobs at 1.7% (Acevedo). The surplus of teachers in 1967, four times what the Ministry could hire, is one direct sign of it. Teachers, students and the urban educated were the groups at the centre of the protests of 1968, 1971 and 1972.

**It reframes the Common Market and the 1969 war.** Import substitution under the Common Market was the period's industrial policy, and it did diversify the economy (Hausmann and Rodrik). Two-thirds of industrial exports went to the Common Market in the mid-1970s (Acevedo). The 1969 war and the crisis of the Common Market cut off the market on which the more complex part of the economy depended.

**It links to the tax bargain.** The industrial exemptions of 1952, 1961, 1969 and 1974, which we count in the coalition pillar, were the instruments of that industrial policy. The question for our study is what they bought: whether the tax spending on industry moved the export basket toward more complex products, or mainly protected the incomes of the coffee and banking families who owned the new firms.

**It is testable for 1962–1979.** The trade data begin in 1962. With them we can compute, year by year, El Salvador's diversity, its complexity, its EXPY and its position in the product space, and set these against the education series, the teachers' payroll and the coffee cycle.

**Limits.** PRODY and the complexity index weight products by the incomes of the countries that export them, measured in later years, so applying them to the 1960s assumes the ranking of products was stable. The framework is about growth, not politics: the link from frustrated educated groups to polarization is our hypothesis, to be tested with the political record. And for the 1950s there are no product-level data; we use simpler measures such as the shares of coffee and cotton in exports.
