This note reviews three founding texts of development economics, read against the research question: whether the fiscal and monetary management of a small, open, agro-exporting economy contributed to the political polarization that led to the civil war. They are the intellectual frame of the modernization the military regime undertook from 1948. Prebisch's 1950 report is the founding document of ECLA's structuralism, which shaped the Hoselitz mission (1954) and the Central American integration strategy promoted by Jorge Sol Castellanos.

| Text | What it adds |
|:--|:--|
| **Prebisch (1950)**, *The Economic Development of Latin America and its Principal Problems* (ECLA) | Centre and periphery; the terms of trade; **a rule for anti-cyclical fiscal policy in an export economy, and why it is politically hard to follow**; the prediction that industrialization turns rural slumps into urban unemployment |
| **Singer (1950)**, "The Distribution of Gains between Investing and Borrowing Countries" | Export sectors as enclaves; manufacturing as a source of education and skills; booms that give the means to industrialize but remove the incentive |
| **Hirschman (1958)**, *The Strategy of Economic Development* | Linkages, and why coffee has few; public works ahead of demand; "trickling-down" and "polarization" effects between regions, which describe the Common Market and Honduras |

# 1. Prebisch (1950)

**Reference.** Economic Commission for Latin America (Raúl Prebisch) (1950). *The Economic Development of Latin America and its Principal Problems*. Lake Success, NY: United Nations, E/CN.12/89/Rev.1, 27 April 1950. Original text in Spanish (1949).

**Centre and periphery.** The world economy is divided between industrial centres, where technical progress originates, and a periphery that exports primary products. The fruits of technical progress have stayed mainly in the centres. The ratio of primary-product prices to manufactured prices fell 36.5% between the 1870s and the 1930s (British data, p. 12). Industrialization is therefore the way for the periphery to capture the fruits of technical progress.

**The cycle at the periphery.** At the centres, investment drives the cycle. At the periphery, **exports are the dynamic factor**, and the periphery cannot regulate them (p. 49). Two consequences matter for us:

- **Industrialization does not remove vulnerability to the cycle; it makes the cycle more visible.** "In a country that is essentially agricultural, depressions manifest themselves more in the fall of rural income than in unemployment … This cannot be expected when relatively large industrial masses are concentrated in the cities: in that case the cyclical unemployment problem would assume serious social complications" (p. 50). Written in 1949, this anticipates El Salvador in the 1970s: an urban, industrial and educated population facing coffee busts.
- **The compensatory fiscal rule, and its political problem.** The usual remedy is to vary public works against exports. In the upswing, tax revenue rises and bonds sell easily, but "the State should not only abstain from using these additional resources to increase public investments, but should restrict them … Thus during the upswing resources would be accumulated in anticipation of bad times or used to repay bank credit extended during the preceding contraction." Prebisch immediately doubts it can be done: development needs exceed resources, so asking governments to save for "their successors" "would mean making the success of anti-cyclical action depend on attitudes not always compatible with sound political interest" (pp. 50–51).

**His alternative: imports and reserves (pp. 51–55).** Instead of public works, Prebisch proposes to keep employment high through the cycle by reducing the import coefficient (import substitution through tariffs), postponing imports of durable consumer and capital goods in downswings, using monetary reserves and international lending to cover the gap in a deep slump, and, if necessary, exchange control to ration non-essential imports. Easy credit would force a choice between devaluation and exchange control.

**Relevance to our findings.**

- Our series shows that El Salvador did **not** follow the compensatory rule: in booms, 94 centavos of each extra colón of revenue were spent. Prebisch explains why this was the politically natural outcome, and names the alternative: save the windfall or repay bank credit. The 1943 coffee duty earmarked for debt repayment (Turcios) was a step in that direction; the 1970s boom was not.
- The monetary side of our question is Prebisch's: keeping the peg at ₡2.50 per dollar, the reserves, the Central Bank's credit to the Treasury from 1969, and the choice between restricting imports, borrowing and adjusting spending when coffee fell.

# 2. Singer (1950)

**Reference.** Singer, H. W. (1950). "The Distribution of Gains between Investing and Borrowing Countries." *American Economic Review* 40(2), Papers and Proceedings: 473–485.

**Export sectors as enclaves.** Export sectors built by foreign investment often became "an outpost of the economies of the more developed investing countries". The secondary and multiplier effects of the investment took place where the investment came from, not where it was located (p. 475). In El Salvador coffee was domestically owned, but its processing, credit and export were concentrated in a few families, a domestic version of the same problem.

**Manufacturing as a source of skills.** The most important contribution of an industry may be "its effect on the general level of education, skill, way of life, inventiveness, habits, store of technology, creation of new demand". Manufacturing provides "the growing points for increased technical knowledge, urban education, the dynamism and resilience that goes with urban civilization" (pp. 476–477). This is the economic premise of the regime's linking of industrialization and education.

**The terms of trade.** Since the 1870s prices have moved against sellers of food and raw materials. Technical progress in manufacturing raised producers' incomes, while technical progress in primary production lowered prices, so the industrial countries "have had the best of both worlds" and the underdeveloped countries "the worst of both worlds" (pp. 477–480).

**Booms and slumps (p. 482).** "Good prices for their primary commodities … give to the underdeveloped countries the necessary means for importing capital goods and financing their own industrial development; yet at the same time they take away the incentive to do so, and investment … is directed into an expansion of primary commodity production." In slumps the desire for industrialization sharpens just as the means disappear: countries risk "failing to industrialize in a boom because things are as good as they are, and failing to industrialize in a slump because things are as bad as they are." This is the dilemma of the coffee boom of 1976–77 and the bust of 1978.

# 3. Hirschman (1958)

**Reference.** Hirschman, Albert O. (1958). *The Strategy of Economic Development*. New Haven: Yale University Press.

**The argument.** What poor countries lack is not resources but the ability to mobilize them: a "binding agent" for decisions to invest (ch. 1). Development should therefore proceed through deliberate imbalances that create pressures and induce further decisions ("unbalanced growth", chs. 3–4), rather than through balanced investment on all fronts.

**Linkages: why coffee does little on its own (ch. 6, pp. 108–110).** Activities stimulate others through backward linkages (demand for inputs) and forward linkages (supply of inputs to other activities). "Agriculture in general … [is] characterized by the scarcity of linkage effects." Processing such as the milling of coffee is a "satellite" activity whose value added is small relative to the product. Primary exports "slip out of a country without leaving much of a trace in the rest of the economy", although they finance imports, which can then induce domestic industry (ch. 7, "The Role of Imports in Inducing Industrial Development"). This is the mechanism of import substitution in El Salvador: coffee earnings financed imports, whose markets industry then took over behind tariffs.

**Public works ahead of demand (ch. 5).** Hirschman distinguishes social overhead capital (roads, power, ports) from directly productive activities. Development can proceed "via excess capacity" of social overhead capital, building ahead of demand to induce private investment, or "via shortage". The public works of the 1950s, the Lempa hydroelectric scheme (CEL), the port of Acajutla and the roads, were a strategy of development via excess capacity. This is part of what our development pillar measures.

**Trickling-down and polarization effects (ch. 10, pp. 187–188).** When one region (the "North") industrializes, it affects the lagging region (the "South") in two ways:

- *Trickling-down* effects: the North buys from and invests in the South, and absorbs its underemployed.
- *Polarization* effects: Southern industries are depressed by Northern competition; the South "is also likely to make a bad bargain since it will now have to buy Northern manufactures, produced behind newly erected tariff walls, instead of similar goods previously imported from abroad at lower prices"; and the South loses its skilled people and capital to the North.

Written in 1958, before the Common Market, this describes its later politics. Within the Common Market, El Salvador was the industrializing "North" and Honduras the "South", which bought Salvadoran manufactures behind the common tariff while hundreds of thousands of Salvadoran migrants worked its land. The Honduran grievance over the unequal gains of integration was one of the causes of the 1969 war. **Hirschman's "polarization" is an economic, regional concept and must be kept distinct from the political polarization of our research question**, but the two meet in 1969.

# 4. What the three texts add to the project

1. **The rule the regime did not follow.** Prebisch states the anti-cyclical fiscal rule for an export economy, save or repay debt in the boom, and predicts why governments would not follow it. Our finding that windfalls were spent almost in full, and that busts were met by cuts until 1968 and by borrowing from 1971, can be framed as a test of Prebisch's rule and his political caveat.
2. **The social cost of the cycle rises with industrialization.** Prebisch predicted that industrialization would turn rural slumps into urban unemployment with "serious social complications". Combined with Acevedo's finding that industry created few jobs, this links the coffee cycle to the urban groups at the centre of the protests.
3. **Industry, education and skills were one project.** Singer's "growing points" for "urban education" and skills is the economic idea behind the development pillar, and behind the regime's investment in teachers and the University.
4. **The Common Market contained the seeds of 1969.** Hirschman's polarization effects describe the imbalance between El Salvador and Honduras that the 1969 war ended.
5. **The monetary side.** Prebisch's analysis of reserves, the import coefficient and exchange control gives the frame for the monetary half of our question: how the Central Bank, under a fixed exchange rate, handled the coffee cycle.
