This note brings together the reviews of three background readings, prepared for the narrative briefing for the research assistants. Each is read against the research question: whether the fiscal and monetary management of a small, open, agro-exporting economy contributed to the political polarization that led to the civil war.

# 1. Ching, "El Salvador" (2020)

**Reference.** Ching, Erik (2020). "El Salvador." In Robert H. Holden (ed.), *The Oxford Handbook of Central American History*, ch. 20, pp. 545–566. New York: Oxford University Press. https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780190928360.013.20 . File: *Literature/History_and_Politics/Ching_2020_El_Salvador_Oxford_Handbook.pdf*.

**What it is.** A survey chapter on El Salvador from independence to 2019, with a historiographic slant: for each period it sets out the scholarly debate and where new archival work has moved it. The period 1931–1979 takes about three pages (pp. 552–554), and the origins of the war about two (pp. 554–556). The notes (pp. 561–566) are a good guide to the recent literature.


## 1.1 The argument

**Guns rather than schools.** The chapter opens with a budget. In 1910 El Salvador budgeted ₡800,000 for education and ₡4,500,000 for defence; the Costa Rican Ricardo Jiménez told a Salvadoran official that the country would be better off reversing the figures (pp. 545–546, citing Córdova's memoir). Ching's explanation is the comparative-development literature (Williams, Mahoney, Holden): an ethnically divided society, where a dominant minority sees any gain for the majority as its own loss, finds it hard to agree on public goods. He calls this the "zero-sum game of ethnic politics". The ethnic line faded by the mid-twentieth century, but the division between haves and have-nots remained, and "those who had wealth and power spent money on guns rather than on schools" (p. 545).

**Choices, not destiny.** Ching closes with Mainwaring and Pérez-Liñán: the war was not the inevitable result of poor economic conditions, since several neighbours had worse indicators and did not go to war. The difference lay in politics and in the decisions of the actors. "The same priorities that caused El Salvador to have the skewed budgetary priorities in 1910 were those that sent the country hurtling down the path to war in the 1970s" (pp. 560–561).

## 1.2 Where it bears on our question

**The budget as the record of priorities.** Ching uses a budget comparison to frame a century of Salvadoran history, which is the premise of this project. But our series shows that **in 1948–1979 the shares were reversed**: development rose to about half of spending and education to 27% in 1971, while security fell to 6.5% in 1976. On our evidence the military regime did spend on schools. The question for this period is therefore not *whether* it spent on development but *how*: the terms of the teachers' expansion, who was taxed to pay for it, and what was protected when coffee fell. Ching's 1910 figures are a useful benchmark for the long-run shift, alongside Pleitez's averages for 1827–1948.

**The "schizophrenia" of military rule.** This is the closest statement in the literature of the contradiction between the development and security pillars:

- On one side, the regimes were authoritarian dictatorships "designed to violently maintain the status quo against any form of autonomous, mass-based social change". They had the army and the National Guard, the Hacienda Police and the Judicial Police, and ORDEN, a paramilitary organization that recruited "as many as one hundred thousand" rural people to watch their neighbours for "communism" (p. 554).
- On the other side, military rule "revolved around this notion of reform". The language of reform became more strident with each government, the scapegoating of the economic elite more explicit, and the scale of reform larger, up to Molina's 1976 land reform, abandoned under elite opposition despite his "ni un paso atrás" (p. 554).
- Ching gives the **1968 education reform** as "another prototypical example of this schizophrenia" (note 26, citing Lindo-Fuentes and Ching, 2012).

**Why the regime reformed: consent and the tandas.** Ching gives two reasons (pp. 553–554):

1. The military governments sought consent. They presented themselves as nationalist defenders of ordinary people against communists abroad and greedy elites at home. Martínez set the precedent in 1932.
2. The officer corps was organized in *tandas*, graduating classes loyal to one another. Junior tandas blocked from promotion used reformist rhetoric to justify coups against their seniors (1948, 1961, 1979), and once in office had to live up to it, at least for a while.

**The military and the elite: a "rocky marriage".** The standard reading, an alliance in which the oligarchy let officers govern in exchange for protection, is "to an extent" correct. But officers and elites came from different worlds. Most officers were of humble origin and more nationalist, and some were willing to countenance reform. The elite wanted a cheap rural labour force without government protection, distrusted the officers and guarded against any reform touching its economic freedoms. They were united by anticommunism and by holding back mass mobilization (pp. 552–553). This qualifies Stanley's "protection racket" and fits our coalition pillar: the elite's veto worked mainly through taxes and reforms withdrawn (Tracks A and D), not through the budget.

**A recurrent cycle.** After 1931, five episodes followed the same pattern: 1944, the coup of October 1960, the elections of 1972 and 1977, and the coup of 1979. Each was a mostly non-violent, mass-based movement for democracy and reform, suppressed by officers and intransigent civilian elites who eventually financed death squads. "When nonviolent demands for change are repeatedly forestalled, support for violent alternatives is likely to grow" (p. 552). Zamora's list of crises is similar (1944, 1948, 1960, 1972, 1979), but he ties them to coffee prices, urban mobilization and US policy.

**The origins of the armed opposition.** The war came from the joining of urban guerrillas and urban workers with a mass mobilization of the peasantry (p. 554). The guerrillas of the 1970s were urban, often university-educated. In the countryside they found peasants already organized, many through the Church's training centres for lay catechists after Vatican II and Medellín (1968): Chávez's "peasant intellectuals". Repression of their questioning turned some toward self-defence and militancy (pp. 555–556). The educated groups produced by modernization appear here in the countryside as well as the city.

## 1.4 Sources from the notes worth obtaining

- **Lindo-Fuentes and Ching (2012),** *Modernizing Minds in El Salvador: Education Reform and the Cold War, 1960–1980*: the core source on the 1968 reform and the teachers, and the reference for H1 and H2.
- **Williams and Walter (1997),** *Militarization and Demilitarization in El Salvador's Transition to Democracy*: the military as an institution. Walter is also the source of Zamora's spending series.
- **Cáceres Prendes (1995),** "Discourses of Reformism: El Salvador, 1944–1960" (PhD diss., University of Texas): the reformist discourse of the 1950s.
- **Huezo Mixco (2017),** *Desafiando los poderes: acción colectiva y frentes de masas en El Salvador, 1948–1980*: the mass organizations, our full period.
- **Gould (2019),** *Solidarity under Siege: The Salvadoran Labor Movement, 1970–1990*: unions and wages.
- **Chávez (2017),** *Poets and Prophets of the Resistance*: intellectuals and the origins of the war.
- **Holden (2004),** *Armies without Nations*, and **Mahoney (2001),** *The Legacies of Liberalism*: the comparative frame.
- **Mainwaring and Pérez-Liñán** on democracy and dictatorship in Latin America: the argument that the war was a political choice, not an economic inevitability.
- **Bosch (1999),** *The Salvadoran Officer Corps*: the tanda system.

## 1.5 Use in the briefing

- **The frame:** the "schizophrenia" of military rule is the development–security contradiction in the historians' own words, and the 1968 education reform is its example. The "rocky marriage" qualifies the alliance between officers and elite.
- **The question:** Ching and Mainwaring and Pérez-Liñán place the cause in political choices, not economic conditions. Our project measures one of those choices, how the State handled the coffee cycle, year by year.
- **A contrast our data can show:** in 1910 the budget bought guns rather than schools. Between 1948 and 1979 the regime bought both, and the shares favoured schools until the 1978 bust.


# 2. Turcios, *Larga dictadura* (2021)

**Reference.** Turcios, Roberto (2021). *Larga dictadura*, 2º fascículo. San Salvador: Editorial Universidad Evangélica de El Salvador (UEES). 22 pp. ISBN 978-99961-331-7-6. Part of the UEES research project "Evolución constitucional salvadoreña en el siglo XX". File: *Literature/History_and_Politics/Turcios/Turcios_2021_Larga_Dictadura.pdf*.

**What it is.** A short essay on the transition of 1944: the fall of Martínez in May, the constitutional debate over the 1886 and 1939 texts, and the "reversal" of the transition by the dictatorship's heirs, ending with Osmín Aguirre's coup in October. It is built on the press of the time (*La Prensa Gráfica*, *El Diario de Hoy*), the Diario Oficial and memoirs. Its argument is in the title: the dictatorship left a "solid sediment" in the army and the Assembly that renewed it and stopped the democratic wave with repression (p. 4).


## 2.1 What matters for our question: the coffee taxes of 1937 and 1943

This is the most useful part for the project, and it comes from primary sources (pp. 19–21).

**1937: relief for the growers in a bust.** When coffee prices fell in November 1937, the government cut the coffee export duty by two-thirds, "in a spirit of justice for the benefit of the producer", knowing it would unbalance the budget. At the same time it suspended service on the external debt and kept funding the rest of the administration with insufficient revenue (quoting the 1943 bill's preamble). **In the bust, the State took the loss and protected the growers.**

**1943: a coffee duty tied to the world price, earmarked for debt.** As wartime coffee agreements raised prices, the Executive sent the Assembly a new coffee export tax in October 1943:

- The rate followed a "parabolic scale" on the spot price of Santos No. 4 coffee in New York. At US$9.00, the tax would be ₡0.13 per 46 kg, about what the average grower would pay in income tax.
- **Its yield was earmarked "exclusively" for servicing the State's debts,** above all the external debt. The preamble's reasoning is explicitly countercyclical: "it is precisely in times of abundant means of payment that the burden of debts contracted by previous generations should be lightened". It also argued that debt amortization should be "to some extent a function of coffee prices", so that higher prices bring faster repayment without unbalancing the balance of payments, "thus guaranteeing exchange-rate stability".
- The Assembly approved it on 11 November 1943. A transitory article set the duty for the current crop at ₡3.16 per 46 kg exported (*Diario Oficial*, 12 November 1943, p. 3229).

**1943: an excess-profits tax that coffee was exempt from.** The Executive also proposed a tax on extraordinary profits, on net profit after costs: 15% up to ₡10,000; 20% from ₡10,000 to ₡50,000; 25% from ₡50,000 to ₡100,000; 30% above ₡100,000. It was additional to income tax, but exempted income from labour, **income from coffee production**, and incomes under ₡3,000 (*LPG*, 11 November 1943). Turcios reads it as possibly a manoeuvre to divide the propertied groups, taxing one while threatening all, and says the turn Martínez later claimed "was not carried out" (p. 21). Whether the excess-profits tax was enacted is not clear from the text.

**Why it matters.**

- **A precedent for Decree 836.** The progressive coffee export duty of 1950, and the exemption of coffee income from income tax, have a direct antecedent in 1943. Both the duty linked to the world price and the exemption of coffee from the profits tax were already there. Our account of the 1950 tax bargain (Track A) should start in 1937–1943.
- **A precedent for our counterfactual.** The 1943 preamble states in the government's own words the logic of a stabilization rule: save the windfall by paying down debt when coffee is high. Our research agenda proposes a coffee stabilization fund counterfactual for 1976–78. The Martínez government made the argument in 1943, and the military governments of the 1970s did the opposite. This is worth a paragraph in the briefing and the paper.
- **The asymmetry.** In 1937 the bust was absorbed by the Treasury and the external creditors, not by the growers. In 1943 the boom was taxed for debt. The test for our period is whether the same rule held after 1950: our series shows windfalls spent almost in full, and busts met by cuts until 1968 and by borrowing from 1971.

## 2.2 Other points for the background

**Social spending under Martínez was small.** The Junta de Defensa Social distributed 2,099 lots by 1938. By 31 December 1942 it had spent more than ₡3M buying 28 properties (53,800 manzanas) to divide into parcels, and built some two hundred houses in San Salvador (119 in Colonia El Bosque, 40 in Colonia San Vicente, others elsewhere in the capital) (*LPG*, 6 February 1943). Turcios: these amounts could not change access to land or housing (pp. 13–14). Martínez also cited the Cajas Rurales for cheap credit.

**Health and the countryside.** Turcios summarizes Arturo Romero's 1943 articles on the Hospital Rosales: of 41 patients in one ward, 31 were day labourers earning 25 to 50 centavos a day, 34 were visibly anaemic, and malaria was the main cause of admission. Romero's remedies were rural doctors, a malaria campaign and a minimum living wage for the peasant; he noted that the health service's budget could not fund "a tenth" of its own programme (pp. 15–16). This is the baseline that the development pillar of the 1950s started from.

**The turn to the United States.** The German general Eberhard Bohntedt directed the Military School and Academy from 1938 to 1939. From May 1941, under an agreement signed that March, the director was a US officer, Colonel Robert L. Christian. Officers who had trained in Italy in 1938 (including Óscar Osorio) gave way to young officers trained in US academies (pp. 11–12). The US legation under Walter Thurston (from January 1943) became, in Miguel Mármol's words, "a kind of super-government" (p. 18). This is the start of the relationship with Washington that Zamora and Ching place at the centre of military rule.

**The heirs of the dictatorship.** After the failed April 1944 revolt, critical officers were shot or fled, so the army that managed the transition was purged in favour of Martínez's heirs. The student leaders withdrew from the succession talks after winning: "we have fulfilled our mission, now we return to our studies" (Fabio Castillo, p. 9). The Decree of the Three Powers (4 July 1944) restored the 1886 Constitution, but the heirs consolidated their positions between May and October (pp. 21–22).

## 2.3 Use in the briefing and the research

- **Briefing, background section:** 1944 as the first of the cycles of mobilization and reversal that Zamora and Ching describe, and the military's turn to the United States from 1941.
- **Briefing, revenue section:** the 1937 and 1943 coffee taxes as the antecedent of Decree 836 and of the coffee exemption.
- **For the research assistants (Diario Oficial):**
  - the 1937 decree reducing the coffee export duty by two-thirds;
  - the coffee export tax decree of 11 November 1943 (DO 12 November 1943, p. 3229) and the duty set for each later crop;
  - whether the excess-profits tax of 1943 was enacted, and its yield;
  - the yields of the 1943 coffee duty and the debt it serviced, from the Memorias de Hacienda of 1943–1950 if they can be found.


# 3. Acevedo, Growth in El Salvador, 1950–2000 (2003)

**Reference.** Acevedo, Carlos (2003). *El crecimiento económico en El Salvador durante la segunda mitad del siglo XX*. Discussion paper, Inter-American Development Bank, Regional Operations Department II, Washington, D.C., August 2003. Part of a regional project on the determinants of growth in Central America and the Dominican Republic, coordinated by Manuel R. Agosin, Roberto Machado and Paulina Nazal. The author was then director of macroeconomics at FUSADES. File: *Literature/Development_Models/Acevedo_2003_Crecimiento_El_Salvador_Siglo_XX.pdf*.

**What it is.** An economic history of growth, 1950–2000, in two parts: a periodization of the economy (pp. 3–10), and a growth-accounting and error-correction analysis of total factor productivity (TFP) (pp. 10–24). For our project the first part supplies the macroeconomic setting, and the second part has two results that bear on the research question.


## 3.1 The macroeconomic setting, 1950–1980

**Growth by period (real GDP, average per year; Table 3).**

| Period | GDP | TFP (adjusted series) |
|:--|--:|--:|
| 1950–54 | 4.5% | 1.5% |
| 1955–59 | 4.9% | 1.5% |
| 1960–64 | 6.3% | 3.2% |
| 1965–69 | 4.8% | 1.1% |
| 1970–74 | 4.5% | −0.3% |
| 1975–79 | 3.1% | −2.3% |
| 1950s / 1960s / 1970s | 4.7% / 5.6% / 3.8% | 1.5% / 2.2% / −1.3% |

The 1950s and 1960s were the fastest-growing decades of the half-century, apart from the post-war rebound of 1990–95. **Productivity growth turned negative in the 1970s,** before the war: in the second half of the decade output grew only through capital accumulation (capital stock +7.2% a year). Acevedo calls 1971–1980 the "exhaustion" of the agro-export and import-substitution model.

**The agro-export platform.** Coffee was joined by cotton (1.5% of exports in 1945, 15% in 1960) and sugar, which gained part of Cuba's US quota in the 1960s. In the early 1950s Salvadoran coffee yields were the highest in the world, and by 1957–58 so were cotton yields (p. 5, citing Bulmer-Thomas). Capital accumulated in agro-exports financed much of the early industrialization, which the State supported with tax exemptions, discretionary tariffs and infrastructure (p. 4).

**Import substitution: growth without jobs.**
- Manufacturing grew 8.1% a year in 1960–70, and manufactures rose from 5.6% to 28.7% of exports. By the mid-1970s almost two-thirds of industrial exports went to the Common Market (pp. 4–5).
- But industry created jobs at only 1.7% a year in 1961–71, while open unemployment in the sector rose from 5% to 9%. Industrial workers fell from 13% to 11% of the labour force, 1960–70, and almost half of them processed coffee, cotton and sugar (pp. 5–6).
- 75% of industry and 40% of the urban population were in metropolitan San Salvador by the early 1970s (p. 6).
- Imports of intermediate and capital goods added pressure on the balance of payments, so import substitution did not reduce external vulnerability (p. 6).

**The countryside.**
- Land Gini of 0.83 in 1970, the highest in Central America (Gordon, 1989). Durham: land per farmer fell from 7.4 ha (1892) to 0.4 ha (1971), more than 80% of it due to concentration and only about 15% to population growth (p. 5).
- In the 1950s Ivory Coast coffee workers earned 20% more in real terms than Salvadoran ones (White, 1973). Rural underemployment was 47% in the mid-1970s, the highest in Latin America (USAID, 1977).
- Between 150,000 and 300,000 Salvadorans were working land in Honduras when the 1969 war broke out, 15–20% of Honduras's labour force (Bulmer-Thomas, 1987). **This is a stock of migrants, not the number expelled:** our notes give 60,000–130,000 returnees.
- About 250,000 Salvadorans emigrated in the 1970s (MIPLAN), before the war-time exodus of the 1980s (p. 13).

Acevedo's conclusion for the period: the social disparities produced by the model, worsened by the exhaustion of the Common Market and the 1969 war, "began to give way to the political contradictions that would lead to the outbreak of the crisis in the seventies" (p. 6).

## 3.2 Two results that bear on our question

**Education did not raise productivity.** In the error-correction model (1962–2000), secondary enrolment enters with a significant *negative* sign in the TFP equation (Table 8). Acevedo cites Pritchett's three explanations (p. 24):

1. schooling was too low to create human capital;
2. **the returns to education fell quickly as supply expanded while the demand for educated labour stagnated;**
3. the institutional setting made educational accumulation unproductive.

The second explanation is the mechanism in our frame: the State expanded education for a modernizing economy, but import substitution created few jobs, so the educated groups it produced found no place. Our education note found the same thing for teachers: by 1967 the private training schools produced about four times as many teachers as the Ministry could hire. **This is a hypothesis to develop, not a finding**: the result rests on a national enrolment index and covers 1962–2000, so it is dominated by the war years.

**Fiscal deficits as a stabilizer.** The lagged Central Government deficit has a positive, significant coefficient (regression for 1971–2000). Acevedo suggests that "until the late eighties, public spending was a not insignificant engine of growth, acting in several periods as a 'stabilizer' of the economy" (p. 24). Our series qualifies this for 1952–1979: spending followed revenue up in booms, so it was procyclical. From 1971 it was financed with debt in busts, which would have cushioned demand in 1971, 1975 and 1978. The two readings are consistent for the 1970s and differ for the earlier decades, which his regression does not cover.

Also from the model: the terms of trade raise TFP and inflation lowers it. The war dummy runs from **1977**, not 1980, so the author dates the start of the conflict's economic effect to 1977.

## 3.3 Data sources for our gaps

The paper names the sources it combined, which are leads for the GDP and price series we lack before 1968 and 1974:

- **GDP and investment:** Banco Central de Reserva from 1970 (1990 prices), combined with **CEPAL and World Bank** series for 1950–70.
- **Labour force:** World Bank, 1950–1990.
- **Inflation (CPI), Central Government deficit, terms of trade, real exchange rate, openness:** the **Global Development Network Growth Database**, 1960–98.
- Earlier growth studies: Harberger (1993), Morales (1998, already in the repository as the IMF working paper) and Edwards (1999).

## 3.4 Use in the briefing

- **The external setting:** growth of 4.7% and 5.6% a year in the 1950s and 1960s on the agro-export platform and the Common Market; productivity falling in the 1970s before the war.
- **The contradiction:** import substitution grew output but not jobs, and the expansion of education met stagnant demand for educated labour. This links the development pillar to the frustration of the groups it created.
- **Data tasks:** the CEPAL and World Bank national accounts for 1950–70, and the GDN Growth Database for prices and the deficit.
