## Framing thoughts (to discuss before drafting)

**1. The starting point and the project.** In 1948 El Salvador was a simple, rural agro-export economy, and the State was financed at the border: export duties (coffee) and taxes on imports and consumption. The Osorio–Lemus project, framed by ECLA's structuralism, set out to diversify and industrialize that economy and to build a technical cadre and an urban middle class.

**2. ECLA as diagnosis, El Salvador's version as practice.** Prebisch and Singer argued that the terms of trade move against primary exporters, so the periphery must industrialize. El Salvador adopted the instruments: tax exemptions (1952), protective tariffs (late 1950s) and Central American integration. Dada Hirezi's critique is that it did so without the structural change ECLA had in mind: integration took the free-trade form the local bourgeoisie preferred, and the coffee surplus was moved to industry within the same propertied groups. **The note can test this directly:** did the revenue base, the export basket and the structure of production change between 1948 and 1960?

**3. "Complexity" in the 1950s.** Hausmann and Hidalgo's measures need product-level trade data, which start in 1962. For the 1950s the note will use simpler indicators: the shares of coffee and cotton in exports, export concentration, manufacturing's share of GDP, and the share of direct taxes in revenue. Hausmann and Rodrik (2005) give the modern El Salvador application and a point of comparison.

**4. The coffee regime: a correction to the working assumption.** The evidence so far suggests the 1950s boom was market-driven (post-war demand and Brazilian supply shortfalls, peaking in 1954), not the result of a cartel or of US generosity.
- The 1940 Inter-American Coffee Agreement was a wartime US import quota.
- After the war the US government seems to have tried to contain prices (a Senate inquiry around 1950 and Korean-War price ceilings); **to be verified.**
- The producer agreements of 1954, 1957, 1958 and 1959 were reactions to falling prices. They worked mainly through Brazil and Colombia withholding coffee, so small producers such as El Salvador gained as free riders, and they did not prevent the 1958–59 fall.
- Oligopoly pricing with the US inside the arrangement came only with the 1962 International Coffee Agreement, in the Alliance for Progress context.

**Implication.** The 1950s modernization rode an open coffee market and took the 1958–59 bust in full, cushioned only by the surplus it had saved. The 1960s had a partial insurance policy, the ICA, that the 1950s never had. That may help explain the relative stability of the Rivera years.

**5. Surface stability, forces in formation.** The Narrative Exercise found that in the 1950s the security budget was not protected and that the 1960 crisis was political rather than fiscal. That fits the view that the 1950s were stable on the surface while the University, teachers, unions and the Christian Democrats were taking shape; those groups became the protagonists of the 1970s.

**6. Where Acemoglu fits (section 4 below).** Acemoglu and Robinson offer a formal language for the project's question:
- **Commitment problem.** Elites concede redistribution when the threat of unrest is high, but concessions are not credible once the threat passes. El Salvador fits: the Directorio's 1961 income-tax reform was diluted within months; the 1976 agrarian reform was withdrawn; the 1977 land tax and the 1978 company-tax gains were followed by enterprise incentives in December 1978.
- **Repression as the alternative.** When credible redistribution is too costly, elites choose repression, which requires an army. Acemoglu, Ticchi and Vindigni show that a military built for repression gains autonomy and can turn on the elite: a frame for Stanley's "protection racket" and for the young officers' coup of October 1979.
- **De jure versus de facto power.** Formal reforms (1950, 1961) can be offset by the persistent de facto power of the coffee elite.
- **Endowments are not fate.** Nugent and Robinson compare coffee economies: smallholder coffee in Costa Rica and Colombia, large estates in El Salvador and Guatemala. Same crop, different institutions and outcomes.
- **Fiscal capacity.** Besley and Persson link the State's capacity to tax to political cohesion: a useful lens on why El Salvador never built a direct-tax base.
