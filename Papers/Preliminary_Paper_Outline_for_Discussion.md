## 2. The argument in one paragraph (to be tested in the draft)

Between 1950 and 1979, El Salvador's military governments built a developmental State on a narrow fiscal base: coffee and trade, with light taxation of wealth and corporate income. The fiscal system transmitted the coffee cycle instead of smoothing it:

- **Booms:** windfalls were spent almost in full.
- **Busts:** spending was cut in the 1950s and 1960s, and from 1971 it was financed with debt.

The windfalls of the 1970s built the civilian State, mainly education, health and public works. But the bargain behind it excluded the groups that the State's own modernization created:

- teachers;
- students;
- urban workers;
- landless rural families no longer able to migrate to Honduras.

When the 1978 bust came, after the safety valve of emigration and the political opening had closed, the State protected security spending and squeezed education. The fiscal accounts do not cause the war. They show how the regime chose to manage scarcity, and whom it chose to protect.

### 2. Literature and framework (4–5 pages)

- **Development thought after 1945:** ECLA structuralism (Prebisch), import substitution and Central American integration; the Hoselitz report as "the bible" of the Lemus government.
- **Commodity regimes:**
  - the coffee economy and its institutions;
  - the International Coffee Agreement (1962–), which supported prices in the 1960s;
  - the price boom of 1976–77.
- **Fiscal procyclicality in Latin America:** Gavin and Perotti (1997); Kaminsky, Reinhart and Végh (2004).
- **Taxation and state formation:**
  - Schneider on Central American tax systems and elites;
  - Besley and Persson on fiscal capacity;
  - Acemoglu and Robinson on elites, institutions and redistribution.
- **El Salvador's road to war:**
  - Stanley, the "protection racket" State;
  - Almeida, protest waves under opening and threat;
  - Lindo-Fuentes and Ching, the education reform and the teachers;
  - Durham, land scarcity and Honduras;
  - Wood;
  - Dada Hirezi and Pleitez on the model and its coalition;
  - Turcios on the 1950s;
  - Gordon on the crisis of the 1970s.
- **The framework:** a fiscal bargain between the military, the coffee and industrial elite, and the new middle groups, tested through who paid, who was paid, and who was protected when money was scarce.
- **The three pillars of the budget (author's decision, 12 November 2026),** the organizing frame of the paper:
  - **Development** (Education, Health, Public Works) buys legitimacy and builds the new middle groups;
  - **Security** (Defensa, Seguridad Pública) buys control;
  - **Agro-export support** (Agricultura) serves the coalition.
  - The balance among the pillars, and how it moves with the coffee cycle, is the budget's record of whom the regime served.
  - **Provisional:** the pillars are ministry-based. The paper states their limits and treats the 1970s turning points with care until they are validated with Diario Oficial and other micro data (Note on Validating the Three Pillars).
  - **Long-run benchmark** (Pleitez): in the agro-export State (averages 1827–1948), security took 31.1% of spending, debt service 25.6% and development 20.5%. In 1948–1979 the shares were security 11.3%, debt 3.6% and development 45.6%. The 1950s were the shift from a security State to a development State.
- **Hypotheses H1–H5 and Tracks A–D,** stated as in the project.

### 5. Results I: who paid — the revenue system (5 pages)

- A State financed at the border:
  - border taxes were 61% of revenue under Osorio, 44% under Rivera, 36% under Sánchez Hernández and 43–44% under Molina and Romero;
  - coffee alone was 27%, then 14%, then 23–26% in the 1970s.
- The 1960s reform:
  - income tax doubled its share (7% to 16%);
  - consumption taxes rose to 23%;
  - import duties were eroded by the Common Market.
- Light taxation of capital:
  - corporate income tax never passed 7% of revenue;
  - the net wealth tax (Vialidad Serie A) was about 2% until 1968;
  - coffee was exempt from income tax from 1950.
- Mass taxes: until 1961 alcohol and cigarettes yielded more than all direct taxes together.
- **Figures:**
  - composition by government (stacked bars);
  - the annual composition with governments shaded;
  - direct against border taxes.

### 6. Results II: who was paid — the three pillars, the cycle and debt (7 pages)

- **The pillars over time (central figure: the three pillars, 1952–1979, against the 1827–1948 benchmark):**
  - development rose from 38% (1952) to about half of spending in 1968–1973;
  - education peaked at 27% in 1971 and fell to 20% by 1979;
  - security fell from 16% (1954) to 6.5% (1976);
  - agro-export support overtook security in 1974–1977;
  - security doubled to 12.7% in 1979.
- **The pillars in booms and busts:** the 1959 adjustment cut public works and Defence; the 1978 bust raised security and cut education.

- **Spending followed revenue up and did not follow it down.** In booms, 94 centavos of each extra colón were spent. In busts, cuts in 1958–59, 1962 and 1968; deficit financing in 1971, 1975 and 1978.
- **Debt took the strain from the late 1960s:**
  - 15–27% of revenue up to 1962;
  - 39% at the end of Rivera;
  - 63% under Sánchez Hernández;
  - 71% in 1979.
  - Debt was cheap (2–5% interest), so the stress was in financing, not in the interest bill.
- **Where the money went:**
  - development rose to about half of spending;
  - education peaked in 1971 (27% on our basis; Pleitez gives 30.4%) and declined after;
  - security fell to 6.5% in 1976 and doubled to 12.7% by 1979.
- **The 1978 bust as the turning point:** security up ₡29.7M, education down ₡2.1M; current transfers and wages rose while real investment fell.
- **The cost of the 1969 war and how it was paid:**
  - war bonds;
  - the emergency tax;
  - the 1970 coffee windfall.
- **Figures:**
  - the three pillars, 1952–1979;
  - the underlying result and debt;
  - the boom and bust table.

### 8. Discussion: what the fiscal record adds (2 pages)

- The regime's choice was not austerity but **inclusion through spending without a tax bargain**. It built schools and hospitals with coffee money and loans, not with taxes on wealth.
- This made the civilian State hostage to coffee and debt. When both failed in 1978, the protection bargain took precedence.
- The fiscal accounts complement Stanley's and Almeida's accounts: they date the turn (1971 for education, 1978 for security) and price it.
- Caveats:
  - association, not causation;
  - the danger of reading history backward;
  - growth in the 1960s was real.
