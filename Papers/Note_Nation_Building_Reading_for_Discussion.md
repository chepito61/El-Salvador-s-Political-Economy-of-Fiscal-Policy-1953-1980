# A Military Regime Interested in Nation Building? A Reading of What the Memorias Show

**Working note for discussion — 13 November 2026.** It responds to the author's observation, made after reviewing the material: *the story the Memorias show is the story of a military regime interested in nation building.* The note asks how far the evidence supports that reading, where it needs qualifying, and what it would change in the article. Nothing in the article has been edited.

---

## 1. Where the evidence supports the reading

The Memorias, read year by year, do show a regime that spent most of its money and effort on building the State and the nation.

- **The budget changed its pillars.** The agro-export State spent about 31% of its budget on security and 26% on debt service (Pleitez's averages for 1827–1948). From the 1950s the military governments spent 38–50% on education, health and public works, and 6.5–16% on security.
- **Education was the largest single effort.** It went from 14% of spending in 1952 to 27% in 1971: schools, teacher training, the 1968 reform, educational television and the University subsidy.
- **Institutions were built in every decade:**
  - **1950s:** CEL and the Lempa dam, social security, housing (IVU), industrial credit, the port of Acajutla, the Litoral highway.
  - **1960s:** the nationalized Central Bank, INSAFI, ANDA, the Common Market, the 1963 income tax law.
  - **1970s:** the Junta Monetaria, housing funds, ISTA, the free zone.
- **A technical cadre ran the economic ministries:** Sol Castellanos, Rochac, Galindo Pohl, Porras, Costa and others.
- **The Memorias themselves are documents of nation building.** They explain policy to the Assembly in the language of development, planning and integration, and they report execution with care.
- **The military's own budget share fell for 25 years.** This is not the budget of a regime that saw its main task as military.

In the terms of the literature, this is close to the idea of the **military as modernizer**, the officers who see themselves as the only national institution able to modernize a backward society (Huntington, *Political Order in Changing Societies*, 1968). It also fits what Pleitez calls the developmental State of 1948–1979.

## 2. Where the reading needs qualifying

The same evidence shows a particular kind of nation building, with three missing pieces.

1. **Nation building without a fiscal contract.**
   - The State was built on coffee and trade taxes, with the income and wealth of the elite lightly taxed: corporate income tax never above 7% of revenue, coffee exempt from income tax, cotton untaxed at the border.
   - The one attempt at a real income tax (1961) was diluted within months.
   - A nation-building State that does not tax its elite depends on the coffee price and on loans. That is the vulnerability of 1978.
2. **Nation building without political inclusion.** Reform went together with repression throughout:
   - the states of siege of 1951 and 1952;
   - the 1956 imposition;
   - the 1960 repression;
   - the frauds of 1972 and 1977;
   - the University takeover of 1972;
   - the Public Order law of 1977.

   The State created new groups (teachers, students, State employees, workers) and then denied them a political voice.
3. **Nation building that stopped at the land question and at the poor.**
   - The agrarian transformation of 1976 was withdrawn.
   - The 1969 returnees from Honduras received no budget line.
   - Landless families rose from 20% to 41% of rural families (1961–1975).
   - The food subsidy (IRA) was small and erratic.
   - The rural majority entered the project mainly through schools and roads, not through land, wages or social security (9% of the labour force contributed to the ISSS in 1979).

**A caution about the source.** The Memorias are the Finance Ministry's report to the Assembly. They record what the State spent and how it wished to be read. They do not record repression, except through the security budget. Its opaque items (the Presidency's lump sums, the extraordinary military budgets of 1968–69) are exactly where the regime's other face appears. The nation-building story is what the State told about itself. It is real in the money, but it is not the whole story.

## 3. A reformulation for the article

The author's reading and the article's thesis can be joined:

> *The Memorias show a military regime that was genuinely committed to building the State: it spent half of its budget on education, health and infrastructure and created most of the country's modern institutions. But it built the State without building a fiscal and political contract. It did not tax the elite that the army protected, and it did not give a voice to the groups the State itself created. Nation building raised expectations that the fiscal base and the political system could not meet, and when coffee and credit failed together in 1978, the regime fell back on its oldest pillar: security.*

This moves the article from "the regime mismanaged the coffee cycle" to **"a nation-building regime without a social contract"**. Read this way, the polarization comes from the gap between the State the regime built and the bargain it was willing to make. The fiscal evidence does not change; its framing does.

**Literature that fits this reframing (to add to the framework):**

- **Huntington (1968),** on the military as modernizers and on political decay when participation outruns institutions.
- **O'Donnell (1973),** *Modernization and Bureaucratic-Authoritarianism*, on modernization producing authoritarian outcomes. El Salvador is not a bureaucratic-authoritarian case in his sense, but the contrast is useful.
- **Stepan (1973),** on the military's "new professionalism" of internal security and national development, the doctrine that tied development to counter-insurgency in the 1960s.
- **Besley and Persson (2011) and Schneider (2012),** on fiscal capacity as the missing link: a State that spends to build the nation but cannot tax.

## 4. Questions for the author

1. **Is "nation building" the right term,** or "State building"? The evidence is strongest for building the State (institutions, services, cadres). Building a nation, a shared political community, is where the record fails.
2. **Should the reframing become the article's central thesis,** with the coffee cycle as the mechanism that exposed the gap? Or should it stay as an interpretive section in the discussion?
3. **Do you see the 1960s (Rivera) as the high point of this project,** with Sánchez Hernández as the turn? The fiscal data fit that reading: the most diversified tax base, rising education, falling security, the political opening of 1964.
4. **Should the article give more space to how the regime described itself** (the language of the Memorias and the presidents' messages) as evidence of the nation-building intent?
