### 2. The argument in one paragraph (to be tested in the draft)

Between 1950 and 1979, El Salvador's military governments built a developmental State on a narrow fiscal base: coffee and trade, with light taxation of wealth and corporate income. The fiscal system transmitted the coffee cycle instead of smoothing it:

- **Booms:** windfalls were spent almost in full.
- **Busts:** spending was cut in the 1950s and 1960s, and from 1971 it was financed with debt.

The windfalls of the 1970s built the civilian State, mainly education, health and public works. But the bargain behind it excluded the groups that the State's own modernization created:

- teachers;
- students;
- urban workers;
- landless rural families no longer able to migrate to Honduras.

When the 1978 bust came, after the safety valve of emigration and the political opening had closed, the State protected security spending and squeezed education. The fiscal accounts do not cause the war. They show how the regime chose to manage scarcity, and whom it chose to protect.

#### 8. Discussion: what the fiscal record adds (2 pages)

- The regime's choice was not austerity but **inclusion through spending without a tax bargain**. It built schools and hospitals with coffee money and loans, not with taxes on wealth.
- This made the civilian State hostage to coffee and debt. When both failed in 1978, the protection bargain took precedence.
- The fiscal accounts complement Stanley's and Almeida's accounts: they date the turn (1971 for education, 1978 for security) and price it.
- Caveats:
  - association, not causation;
  - the danger of reading history backward;
  - growth in the 1960s was real.

# Part 2. Modernization from Above: Coffee, the State and the Forces Beneath the Stability, El Salvador 1948–1960


**Working note — first draft for discussion, 12 November 2026.** This note covers the founding decade of the project of modernization, from the coup of December 1948 to the fall of President Lemus in October 1960. It is written to feed the preliminary paper (*Riding the Coffee Cycle*). Figures come from the Memorias de Hacienda, through the verified yearly notes and the revenue and fiscal workbooks, and from the three works that frame the decade:

- Roberto Turcios, *Autoritarismo y modernización* (1993) and *Rebelión: San Salvador 1960* (2017);
- William Pleitez, chapters 1–2 of his history of El Salvador's development models;
- Héctor Dada Hirezi, *La economía de El Salvador y la integración centroamericana, 1945–1960* (1978).

Page numbers are the printed pages of each work. Points still to verify are marked as such.

---

### Summary

1. **The point of departure was a coffee economy and a coffee State.** Around 1950 coffee was more than 80% of exports (88–89% in 1945–1954). The State's tax base rested on trade.
2. **The modernization of 1948–1960 was financed by a windfall.** The coffee price per quintal multiplied 4.6 times between 1945 and 1954. The new government captured part of it with a progressive export tax (1950), and tax revenue roughly tripled between 1946 and 1951.
3. **The money went to a developmental State:**
   - schools, health and public works took 38–46% of spending;
   - the security apparatus took 13–16%, a share that drifted down;
   - large infrastructure (the Lempa dam, the Litoral highway, the port of Acajutla) was financed with foreign loans and State guarantees.
4. **The tax bargain was written for the coffee and commercial elite.** Coffee paid at the border but was exempt from income tax. Companies paid 2.5–5% against up to 44% for individuals. The 1952 industrial law added exemptions. Taxes on alcohol and cigarettes yielded more than all direct taxes together.
5. **Diversification began but stayed thin.** Cotton rose from 1% to 11% of exports (1945–1957); coffee's share fell from 88% to 66% by 1960. Industry was small: in the early 1950s only 14 factories employed more than 100 workers.
6. **The 1958–59 coffee fall was managed without a fiscal crisis.** The surplus built in the boom absorbed the first year. New consumption taxes and cuts in public works and Defence handled the second. Education was protected.
7. **The stability was partly superficial.** The regime combined social reform with repression:
   - a state of siege in 1951;
   - the mass arrests of September 1952;
   - the 1952 law "in defence of the democratic order";
   - an imposed election in 1956.
   It built a State that created new groups (teachers, students, State employees, professionals, urban workers) without giving them political voice. The crisis of 1960 was political, and these groups were at its centre.

**Proposition for the paper:** the 1950s built both the expectations (a developmental State, education, a middle class) and the fiscal model (coffee, trade and a light tax on capital) whose collision would define the 1970s.

---

### 1. Introduction

The author's reading is that, until the end of the Rivera administration (1967), El Salvador was guided by the project of modernization begun by Colonel Óscar Osorio after the coup of 14 December 1948. The decade looked stable:

- one official party, the PRUD, governed continuously;
- the economy grew on high coffee prices;
- the State built roads, schools, hospitals, a dam and a port.

But forces were born and built up under that surface, and they would become decisive in the 1970s:

- a technical cadre;
- a middle class;
- organized students and workers;
- and a habit of repressing them.

This note asks three questions:

1. What were the economy and the State at the start?
2. What did the reforms try to change, and how were they financed?
3. What was building up beneath the stability?

It adds three things to the existing narrative literature:

- the fiscal record, verified year by year from the Memorias de Hacienda (1952–1960);
- the international setting: development thought and the coffee market;
- a first, descriptive look at diversification.

### 2. The point of departure, c. 1948

#### 2.1 A coffee economy

On the eve of the 1948 coup, El Salvador was a small, densely populated, rural economy built around one export.

- **Coffee was the economy's link to the world.** It was 87.6% of exports in 1945 and 86.8% in 1948 (Dada Hirezi, Cuadro 2-1, pp. 28–29).
- **Production grew slowly; prices did the work.** Coffee export volume rose only from 57.7 thousand tonnes (1945) to 60.3 (1948) and 65.9 (1951). Export value doubled between 1945 and 1948 (₡46.7M to ₡90.4M), and doubled again by 1951 (₡190.1M).
- **Output rose through labour, not land.** Dada Hirezi stresses that the coffee frontier was closed; higher output came from more labour per hectare and from fertilizer (pp. 29–30).

#### 2.2 A State shaped by 1932

The rural rebellion of January 1932 and its repression left two legacies that matter here.

- **A military regime allied to the coffee elite.** Martínez governed until 1944, and the army remained the arbiter of politics afterwards.
- **A policy of containing change.** Dada Hirezi argues that the Martínez regime protected artisans and small workshops, to avoid the unemployment that had fed the 1932 alliance. Examples:
  - a 1939 decree prohibited factories with capital above ₡20,000 in several lines;
  - machinery for sugar mills and cotton gins was banned (DH 1A, ch. 1).
  - Economic policy was used to contain a political threat. This mechanism is the subject of this project.

#### 2.3 Instability, 1944–1948

- Between 1940 and 1948 strikes, mobilizations and military revolts ended the Martínez dictatorship and kept the country unstable (Pleitez, p. 188).
- The coup of 14 December 1948 brought a Consejo de Gobierno Revolucionario (CGR) to power. Its members called it not a "golpe" but "a movement of renewal, to achieve the new organization of the State" (Pleitez, p. 188, citing Turcios).
- The CGR's *Proclama de principios y objetivos* (25 December 1948) promised, among other things:
  - higher living standards, "guaranteeing the growth of production, under the protection of technique";
  - stimulus to private enterprise;
  - social justice;
  - Central American economic ties (Pleitez, p. 189).

### 3. The international setting

#### 3.1 Development thought: ECLA, Prebisch and the Hoselitz mission

The modernization project drew explicitly on the new development economics.

**ECLA's structuralism.** Under Raúl Prebisch, ECLA divided the world economy into a centre and a periphery:

- The **centre** had homogeneous, diversified productive structures.
- The **periphery** was heterogeneous and specialized: technical progress was confined to export activities in unstable markets.

Because productivity grew faster at the centre, and the periphery's terms of trade tended to deteriorate, ECLA concluded that "average incomes diverge over time more intensely than productivities". It prescribed:

- industrialization;
- a larger domestic market;
- regional integration;
- growth poles;
- gradual import substitution, beginning with consumer goods (Pleitez, pp. 194–195, citing Prebisch [1949] and Rodríguez).

**The studies commissioned by the government.** The government commissioned studies that shaped both its organization and its policies (Pleitez, pp. 192–194). The most influential was by the UN industrial expert **Bert F. Hoselitz**, *Industrial Development of El Salvador* (1954). President Lemus later called it "the bible" of his economic policy (Pleitez, p. 194).

Hoselitz started from the observation that El Salvador's main resource was its population. He named the main problems:

- the low standard of living of the majority;
- strong seasonal variation in employment;
- heavy dependence on coffee;
- scarce skilled labour;
- a small domestic market.

Industrialization would:

- create more stable jobs;
- diversify production away from coffee;
- prepare Central American integration;
- reduce dependence on imported consumer goods;
- raise skills and wages.

His prescription was:

- light industry: food, textiles, leather, building materials;
- an input–output study of the links between industries;
- an aggressive education and training plan;
- a Central American free-trade area with a common external tariff;
- a development corporation (Pleitez, pp. 195–196).

**The size of the challenge.** Hoselitz counted only 14 factories employing more than 100 workers, and at most 32 employing more than 50 (Turcios, *Autoritarismo*, p. 43). He also noted the prevailing business maxim that an enterprise was worthwhile only if it promised a 50% profit within a year.

**Integration.** Integration became the strategy's centrepiece. On 16 June 1951, at ECLA's fourth session, the Central American ministers of economy agreed a three-point programme:

- wider markets;
- coordinated investment;
- trade liberalization.

El Salvador's minister, **Jorge Sol Castellanos**, was among its strongest promoters. He said that for El Salvador the Common Market was not an ideal but "part of our vital and economic needs" (Turcios, *Autoritarismo*, pp. 44–45). Bilateral free-trade treaties followed. They led to the multilateral treaties of 1958 and the General Treaty of 1960.

**Dada Hirezi's reading of the outcome.** He notes that the form integration finally took (broad free trade with a high common external tariff, rather than ECLA's planned, reciprocal model) suited the Salvadoran agro-export and import groups. In his reading it consolidated the existing class structure.

#### 3.2 The coffee regime: controls, decontrol and the producers' agreements

The author's view is that commodity exporters operated under arrangements in which prices were set by oligopoly, and that these favoured small countries like El Salvador. The sources support this view for the 1940s and the 1960s. They qualify it for the 1950s.

- **The war years: an assured market, at a capped price.** The Inter-American Coffee Agreement of 1940, backed by the United States, gave Latin American producers export quotas in the US market (ICO, *History*). During the war prices were controlled and the United States was practically the only buyer of Salvadoran coffee (Dada Hirezi, p. 29). The Salvadoran price rose only from ₡23.31 per quintal (1941) to ₡37.24 (1945) (Dada Hirezi, p. 29, n. 2).
- **Decontrol and the post-war boom.** After 1945 world trade shed its wartime restrictions and demand grew faster than supply. Producers organized:
  - the Federación Cafetalera Centroamérica–México (FEDECAME), founded in 1946 under the leadership of the Salvadoran Roberto Canessa, pressed to end the "price ceilings" agreed with the United States (Pleitez, pp. 190–191);
  - Canessa's line in 1954: "If the North Americans want to pay less for a cup of coffee, the Latin American countries … also want to pay less for automobiles, refrigerators and other goods from the United States" (Pleitez, p. 191, n. 9).
- **How far prices rose.** The price of Salvadoran coffee went from ₡37.24 per quintal in 1945 to ₡102.53 in 1950 and ₡170.09 in 1954: an index of 100, 275 and 457 (Dada Hirezi, p. 29; Turcios, *Autoritarismo*, p. 33). Coffee export value rose from ₡46.7M (1945) to ₡230.0M (1954), while production rose only 24% (1945–1955).
- **The 1950s boom was a market windfall, not a cartel price.** The producers' attempts to support prices came as prices fell after 1954, not before:
  - a "gentleman's agreement" on minimum prices among Latin American producers (1954);
  - the Mexico Agreement (1957);
  - the Latin American Coffee Agreement (1958);
  - the first short-term International Coffee Agreement (1959/60), which brought in the African producers (ICO, *History*).
  - These agreements slowed the fall but did not stop it. Turcios's price for Central American milds fell from US$65.20 per quintal (1956–57) to US$41.72 (1959–60) (*Autoritarismo*, p. 145). Dada Hirezi's export price index (1953 = 100) fell from 125 in 1954 to 91 in 1958 and 74 in 1959 (p. 62).
- **The effective cartel came in the 1960s.** The 1962 International Coffee Agreement, with the United States as a member after the policy shift of the Alliance for Progress, set export quotas for all members. It entered into force in 1963 (ICO, *History*). This fits the author's view that prices were supported in the Rivera years.

*To verify:* whether the United States paid premium prices for Latin American agricultural commodities in the early 1950s (the Korean War years, and any US price ceilings on coffee in 1951). The sources consulted so far describe the reverse in the 1940s (ceilings), and a market-driven boom in the early 1950s.

#### 3.3 The Cold War in the isthmus

The decade's politics were shaped by the Guatemalan revolution (1944–1954) and its overthrow in 1954.

- Turcios shows how the Osorio government read Guatemala as a threat.
- In late 1952 El Salvador began to press in the Organization of Central American States (ODECA) for regional action against communism, which "did not need much acuity to be read as an initiative against the Guatemalan government" (*Autoritarismo*, p. 81).
- Anti-communism became a frame for internal repression (section 6).

### 4. The modernization project, 1948–1960

#### 4.1 The 1950 Constitution: a State with a social mandate

The Constitution of 1950 made the State the promoter of national development (Pleitez, pp. 189–190, citing Guidos Véjar).

- Article 2 obliged the State to ensure "liberty, health, culture, economic well-being and social justice".
- Article 135 required the economic regime to respond "essentially to principles of social justice".
- Article 137 guaranteed private property "in its social function".
- Article 182 declared labour a social function and committed the State to "employ all the resources within its reach" to provide employment.

#### 4.2 Institutions

The decade created or activated most of the institutions of the developmental State:

| Area | Institution | Date |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Electricity | **CEL**, the Lempa hydroelectric commission (the 5 de Noviembre dam) | Created by decree in 1945; operating from September 1948 (Pleitez, p. 215, n. 58) |
| Social security | **ISSS** | Social security law of 1949; new law of 1953 (DO 226, t. 161); first clinics from 1954 (Pleitez, p. 225) |
| Housing | **IVU** (urban housing) and the Instituto de Colonización Rural, replacing Mejoramiento Social | Early 1950s; Constitution art. 148 declared housing of social interest (Pleitez, p. 220) |
| Industrial credit | **INSAFOP** (Instituto Salvadoreño de Fomento de la Producción) | 1950s. Pleitez notes it limited itself to medium-term credit for low-risk projects (p. 210) |
| Integration | Bilateral treaties, then the multilateral treaties of 1958 and the General Treaty of 1960 | 1951–1960 |

The technical cadre came from the University and the 1944 generation.

- **Reynaldo Galindo Pohl:** a student leader in 1944, member of the CGR and Minister of Education.
- **Jorge Sol Castellanos:** Minister of Economy.
- **Enrique Porras and Humberto Costa:** Finance.
- **Businessmen in office:** Roberto Canessa and Víctor de Sola, president of CEL from 1950 to 1978 (Pleitez, pp. 198–199; Turcios, *Autoritarismo*, ch. 1).

The University's support for the 1948 movement was explicit at the start. The students' newspaper wrote that "the University Youth has traditionally been combative" (Turcios, *Autoritarismo*, ch. 1). That support did not last (section 6).

#### 4.3 The fiscal reforms

The reforms of 1950–1953 built the tax system that would last, with changes, until 1961.

| Decree | Content | Reading |
|:--|:--|:--|
| D. 836 (CGR), 13 Sep 1950 | Coffee export tax made *ad valorem* and progressive (10% up to US$30 per quintal, rising to 30% on the excess above US$40). Coffee producers exempted from income tax on income from coffee land while the tax applies | Captures the windfall at the border; **builds an income-tax exemption for coffee into the system**; makes revenue more sensitive to the coffee price |
| D. 510, 10 Dec 1951 | Income tax: 28 brackets for individuals (3–44%); two for companies (2.5% and 5%); withholding on wages | A wide gap between individuals and companies |
| D.L. 661, 22 May 1952 | *Ley de Fomento de Industrias de Transformación*: duty-free imports for 5–10 years; income and local tax exemptions | The origin of industrial tax expenditures |
| D.L. 1308, 22 Dec 1953 | *Ley de Vialidad*: "Serie A", a tax on net wealth | A wealth tax at very low rates (about 2% of revenue in the 1950s) |
| February 1959 | New taxes on beer and soft drinks, matches and liquor | Response to the coffee fall; also to offset revenue lost to the new protective tariff (Turcios, *Autoritarismo*, p. 143) |

*Sources: Pleitez, pp. 200–207; Note on the revenue series; Turcios.*

Dada Hirezi summarizes the economic policy of the 1948 regime in nine measures (pp. 37–38):

- the end of wage payment in kind;
- repeal of the restrictions on capital accumulation;
- the higher coffee export tax, "allowing a transfer of the surplus from the agro-export sector to other sectors";
- reform of the income tax;
- roads that opened land to cotton;
- incentives through credit and fiscal advantages;
- a dam that cut electricity prices by 25%;
- bilateral free-trade treaties;
- social security and **urban unions under government control**.

His summary: a State that took greater responsibility for capital accumulation, seeking "industrialization without breaking the internal social equilibrium", the result of "a renewed hegemonic alliance" (p. 38).

#### 4.4 Spending and the large works

The government spent the windfall on a developmental State (section 5.2). The largest works were financed outside the current budget:

- **The 5 de Noviembre dam (CEL):** through the autonomous agency, with State guarantees.
- **The Litoral highway:** an IBRD loan from 1954 (D.L. 1702), with the General Fund paying 57% of the cost over 1955–1962.
- **The port of Acajutla (CEPA):** through the port authority.

See *Note on Loan-Financed Investment 1955–1962*; Turcios, *Autoritarismo*, pp. 93–94.

The boom paid for the current budget and the counterpart funds; the big investments were financed by debt.

### 5. The fiscal record, 1950–1960

#### 5.1 Revenue: a State financed at the border

![Revenue, spending, coffee duty and surplus](/home/claude/el-salvador-s-political-economy-of-fiscal-policy-1953-1980/Papers/charts_1948_1960/fig1_revenue_spending_1950_1960.png){width=6.5in}

Ordinary revenue doubled in the decade, from ₡83.4M (1950) to ₡170.5M (1960), about 7.4% a year. The path followed coffee:

- a jump in 1951, the first year of the new coffee tax;
- a peak of ₡189.5M in 1957;
- a fall of 12% in 1958 and 7% in 1959;
- recovery in 1960.

What the composition shows:

- **Border taxes were 60% of revenue** (import and export duties together): 61% under Osorio and 57% under Lemus.
- **Coffee alone was 25–30% of revenue in the boom.** It fell to 15–16% in 1959–60.
- **Direct taxes were small.** Income tax was 6–10% of revenue. The net wealth tax (Vialidad) brought in ₡1.2–3.8M a year. Successions were the only property tax of weight, and erratic (₡8.8M in 1954, against ₡1.6–4.3M in the other years).
- **Mass taxes outweighed direct taxes.** Alcohol and cigarettes together (₡23–27M a year) yielded more than income and property taxes combined in every year with data (1954–1960).
- **Cotton was almost untaxed.** The second export crop paid no export duty. The only trace is a small tax "Sobre el Algodón" recorded under consumption taxes (₡0.29M in 1956, ₡0.35M in 1957, ₡0.03M in 1958). Cotton exports were then worth ₡39–44M a year.

**The exposure.** With the colón fixed at ₡2.50 per dollar since 1935, the coffee price passed directly into revenue. Turcios estimates that each dollar off the coffee price cost the State about ₡1.19M (*Autoritarismo*, p. 147). The coffee duty fell from ₡54.6M (1957) to ₡35.4M (1958) and ₡24.8M (1959), a loss of ₡29.8M, or 16% of 1957 revenue.

#### 5.2 Spending: the three pillars of the regime's bargain

**The frame (author's decision, 12 November 2026).** This study reads the budget as the three pillars of the bargain on which the military regime rested:

- **Development** (Education, Health and Public Works) buys legitimacy and builds the new middle groups: teachers, students, State employees, professionals.
- **Security** (Defensa and Seguridad Pública, the Defence-controlled apparatus) buys control.
- **Agro-export support** (Agricultura y Ganadería) serves the coalition: research, extension and credit for coffee and cotton.

The balance among the three pillars, and how it changes when coffee rises or falls, is the budget's record of whom the regime chose to serve.

*The pillars are measured here from ministry totals and are provisional. They are to be validated with unit-level and off-budget data from the Diario Oficial and other sources (see* Note on Validating the Three Pillars*).*

**The long-run benchmark: from a security State to a development State.** Pleitez's averages for the agro-export State, 1827–1948 (Tabla 1.15, p. 165), against the import-substitution model, 1948–1979 (Tabla 2.10, pp. 247–248):

|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Security (defence and security) | 31.1% | 11.3% | 12.6–15.9% |
| Development (education + health + public works) | 20.5% | 45.6% | 37.7–45.7% |
| — education | 6.1% | 20.2% | 13.2–19.5% |
| — health | 4.2% | 10.8% | 9.4–11.0% |

The 1950s were the turning point. The old State spent close to six of every ten colones on security and debt. The new one spent more than four on schools, health and roads, and between 13% and 16% on security. **The budget changed its pillars in one decade.**

The rest of the period (figure 5) shows when the balance turns again:

- development peaks at about half of spending around 1968–1973;
- education peaks at 27% in 1971;
- agro-export support overtakes security in 1974–1977, the years of the ISTA and the agrarian programmes;
- security doubles from 6.5% (1976) to 12.7% (1979), back to the level of the late 1950s.

That is the subject of the preliminary paper.

- **Education grew fastest**, from 14% to 19% of spending. Turcios gives the education budget as ₡20.8M in 1956 and ₡32.9M in 1960 (*Rebelión*, p. 168).
- **Security drifted down,** from a peak of 15.9% (1954) to 12.6% (1960), already close to Pleitez's average of 11.3% for 1948–1979.
- **Agro-export support got little:** 3% of spending, in an agricultural country. The coalition was served mainly on the revenue side (the coffee income-tax exemption, untaxed cotton), not through the budget.

#### 5.3 The 1958–59 coffee fall: a managed adjustment

The fall in coffee prices was the decade's one serious shock. It was met in three steps (Consolidated MISI note; Narrative Exercise):

1. **1958, the surplus.** Revenue fell 11.5% and export duties 35%, but spending was held (credits used −0.9%). The ₡17.0M deficit was financed from the Superávit Financiero accumulated in the boom, which fell from ₡45.7M to ₡31.2M.
2. **February 1959, new taxes.** Taxes on beer, soft drinks, matches and liquor, "to maintain the pace of spending" (Turcios, *Autoritarismo*, p. 143).
3. **1959, cuts:**
   - public works −19.9%;
   - Defence −13.3%;
   - education +2.2% and health −1.7%.
   - The Ministry described an "orderly restriction" of spending (Turcios, *Rebelión*, p. 128).

By 1960 revenue had recovered (+8.9%), led by import duties. The surplus rose again to ₡20.3M.

**What the adjustment reveals:**

- **The regime cut investment and the army, not schools.** This is the opposite of the "security protected, development cut" pattern the project looks for. It appears only later (1978).
- **It did not borrow:** direct debt fell from ₡45.8M (1958) to ₡35.6M (1960).
- **It did not tax capital.** The new taxes of 1959 were on mass consumption.

#### 5.4 Debt

Public debt was small and stable:

- ₡30.6M in 1952;
- a peak of ₡45.8M in 1958;
- ₡35.6M in 1960.

That is 15–27% of a year's revenue, with interest about 1% of revenue. The large works were financed with IBRD loans and State guarantees to the autonomous agencies, and their service was paid from the ordinary budget. By 1961–62 the Litoral loans alone cost ₡4.3–4.5M a year (Note on Loan-Financed Investment).

### 6. Diversification and complexity

#### 6.1 The idea

A recent literature measures development by what a country knows how to make.

- **Hausmann, Hwang and Rodrik (2007),** "What You Export Matters": the productivity level of a country's export basket predicts its growth.
- **Hidalgo and Hausmann (2009),** "The Building Blocks of Economic Complexity", and **The Atlas of Economic Complexity (2011):** they treat development as the accumulation of productive capabilities, visible in the variety (diversity) and rarity (ubiquity) of a country's exports.

In that language, the project of 1948–1960 was an attempt to make El Salvador's economy more complex: to move from one crop to a wider basket of agriculture, light industry and regional trade. The idea is close to ECLA's (heterogeneity, the spread of technical progress) and to Hoselitz's (skills, linkages, an input–output study).

#### 6.2 What the data show (descriptive)

The formal indices need detailed trade data that start in the 1960s. For the 1950s, export concentration is the simplest available measure.

| Coffee, % of total exports | 87.6 | 86.8 | 88.9 | 87.6 | 79.3 | 65.7 |

- **Coffee's dominance held through the boom.** It was 87–89% of exports until 1954, and fell to 79% in 1957 and 66% in 1960, mainly because the coffee price fell.
- **Cotton was the one new export of weight.** Area tripled (12.9 to 39.7 thousand hectares, 1945–1957), yields almost tripled and export value rose 55 times (Pleitez, pp. 190–191).
- **The new crop was agricultural, not industrial.** It reorganized the coastal plain and the agrarian structure (Dada Hirezi, p. 29). It was also capital-intensive and seasonal, and it displaced tenants and sharecroppers.
- **Industry remained small** (14 factories with more than 100 workers). Its expansion came after 1960 with the Common Market.

**Reading.** By 1960 the economy had moved from one crop to two, both agricultural and both exposed to world prices. Diversification in the sense of new capabilities (industry, skills, regional trade) was planned in the 1950s and delivered, partly, in the 1960s. A formal complexity measure for 1962–1979 is possible with SITC trade data (UN Comtrade; the Atlas). It belongs to the research agenda.

### 7. Forces building beneath the stability

#### 7.1 A regime that reformed and repressed

The PRUD governed as a single dominant party. It saw itself as "the depository of the revolutionary energies" of 1948 (Turcios, *Autoritarismo*, p. 70). Its record combined social reform with repression:

- **9 March 1951:** the Assembly decreed a state of siege. The Comité de Reorganización Obrero Sindical (CROS) was dissolved, and union leaders were jailed and expelled (p. 73).
- **26 September 1952:** a second state of siege. The Guardia and the Policía Nacional arrested workers, students and democrats; a union historian puts the number above two thousand. It was "one of the largest mass arrests on record" and broke up most of the opposition (pp. 76–80).
- **27 November 1952:** the *Ley de Defensa del Orden Democrático y Constitucional* "institutionalized the political and legal irregularity of the country" (p. 81).
- **1956:** Lemus was elected with 477,000 votes against 11,000 for the PAR and 22,000 for the PAC, in an election marked by imposition. Afterwards the opposition was harassed (p. 72).

Turcios calls September 1952 the turning point: the anti-communist turn of the Osorio government, and the start of a process of loss of social legitimacy (p. 81).

#### 7.2 The new groups the State created

The modernization created the groups that would later oppose the regime:

- **State employees, teachers and professionals**, in a budget where education went from 14% to 19% of spending.
- **Students and the University**, which had supported the 1948 movement and whose leaders had entered the government.
- **Urban workers**, organized in unions under government control (Dada Hirezi, p. 38).
- **The rural poor:**
  - a seasonal labour force for coffee and cotton;
  - tenants displaced by cotton;
  - outside the minimum wage, which the landowners resisted when it was proposed in 1960 (Turcios).
  - For many, emigration to Honduras was the outlet (Dada Hirezi). That outlet would close in 1969.

#### 7.3 The 1960 crisis

The crisis that ended the Lemus government was political (Narrative Exercise, drawing on Turcios, *Rebelión*):

- the split between Osorio and Lemus within the PRUD;
- an opposition front (the Frente Nacional de Orientación Cívica);
- disputed municipal and legislative elections;
- cases of police torture;
- the influence of the Cuban revolution;
- the regime's own repression in August–September 1960, which turned protest into rebellion.

The protests demanded democracy, not an end to austerity. 1960 was a year of economic recovery: a record coffee harvest and manufacturing at its decade high (*Rebelión*, pp. 48–49). The fiscal accounts agree: the deficit nearly closed and the surplus rose in 1960.

**The fiscal squeeze of 1958–59 was background, not the trigger.** The longer-run link is different. Turcios argues that the opposition of 1960 reflected the social changes produced by the decade's modernization (*Rebelión*, p. 320). Those were the groups in section 7.2.

### 8. Reading the decade against the thesis

| Claim | Verdict for 1948–1960 | Evidence |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Coffee financed modernization | **Supported** | Tax revenue tripled 1946–1951 on the export tax; development spending 38–46% |
| Revenue was exposed to coffee | **Supported** | Border taxes 60% of revenue; coffee duty −55% from 1957 to 1959 |
| Security was protected in downturns | **Not supported** | Security share drifted down; Defence cut 13% in 1959 |
| Adjustment fell on development | **Partly** | On public works (−20% in 1959), not on education or health |
| The fiscal squeeze caused the 1960 crisis | **Not supported** | 1960 was a recovery year; the crisis was political |
| The tax bargain favoured coffee and capital | **Supported** | Coffee exempt from income tax; companies 2.5–5%; cotton untaxed at the border; mass consumption taxes larger than direct taxes |
| ISI and integration changed the economy | **Begun, thin** | Cotton to 11% of exports; few factories; integration treaties signed |
| Repression accompanied reform | **Supported** | 1951 and 1952 states of siege; the 1952 law; the 1956 imposition; 1960 |

**Track A (the tax bargain): the starting point.** The fiscal system of the 1950s was designed to capture the coffee windfall at the border without touching the income or wealth of the coffee and commercial elite. That design left the State's finances hostage to the coffee price, and it made the next step (taxing capital) politically costly. The Directorio tried that step in 1961 and partly undid it within months.

**H3 (investment adjusts first): confirmed early.** In the decade's only downturn, public works were cut first.

**The three pillars in the 1950s.** Development rose, security fell and agro-export support stayed small. The bargain was paid for in legitimacy, not in control, and the coalition was served through the tax system.

**The protection bargain (Track C) is not visible in the budget yet.** The army was the regime's political base, but it did not have a protected budget share. Repression was carried out by the regular police and the Guardia, at modest cost.

### 9. Implications for the paper

1. **The 1950s built the expectations.** A State constitutionally committed to social justice, that spent most of its budget on schools, health and roads and that trained a technical cadre, created new groups with new claims.
2. **The 1950s also built the fiscal model.** A tax system based on coffee and trade, with a light tax on capital and mass consumption taxes larger than direct taxes, financed those expectations only as long as coffee paid.
3. **The decade's politics set the pattern of response.** States of siege, laws "in defence of order" and imposed elections were the regime's answer to opposition, even in good years.
4. **The collision came later.** The 1958–59 downturn was absorbed by a surplus and a selective cut. The question for the 1960s and 1970s is what happened when the buffers were thinner, the coffee cycle wilder and the new groups larger and organized.

### 4. Disaggregation: the proposed detail

The broad categories hide the distinctions the argument needs. The proposed structure has about 25 lines.

#### 4.1 Income tax

**Lines:** personal, corporate, other income.

- **1964–1979:** the split is available, except 1971–72 (statements missing from the scans).
- **1953–1963:** the Memorias give only the total; the split would have to come from the Dirección General de Contribuciones Directas. The income tax law of December 1963 (D. 479) introduced the separate reporting.
- **Fines** are included in 1964–68 and excluded from 1969. From 1975 the statements show income-tax fines separately, as non-tax revenue.

#### 4.2 Property and wealth: what Vialidad is

**Vialidad Serie "A" is a net wealth tax.**

- It was created by the Ley de Vialidad, D.L. 1308 of 22 December 1953 (Diario Oficial No. 233, Tomo 161).
- The law that replaced it in 1986 (Ley del Impuesto sobre el Patrimonio, D.L. 553 of 18 December 1986) states that the tax "recae sobre el capital o patrimonio de los sujetos pasivos" and that its rates were "mínimas".
- The 1975–79 statements call it "Impuesto sobre el Capital (Vialidad Serie 'A')".

**Proposed name:** "Net wealth tax (Vialidad Serie A)". Its jump in 1968 (₡5.7M to ₡15.4M) is then a wealth-tax increase, which matters for the tax-bargain argument.

**Proposed lines:**

- net wealth tax (Vialidad A);
- property-transfer tax (*alcabala*; under "other taxes" in the 1950s);
- succession tax;
- gift tax;
- agricultural land tax (1978–79);
- paving levy.

#### 4.3 Exports: coffee, sugar, shrimp and cotton

**Lines:** coffee, sugar, shrimp, cotton (if any), other merchandise.

- **Coffee** dominates throughout.
- **Shrimp:** taxed from 1961 (D. 154).
- **Sugar:** a progressive export tax from November 1974 (D.L. 135), yielding ₡25.6M in 1975 and falling to almost nothing by 1978.
- **"Other merchandise"** reached ₡116.6M in 1978 and fell back to ₡12.5M in 1979; its legal basis is still to be identified.
- **Cotton is the notable case.** It reached about 15% of exports by 1960 (Acevedo, 2003), but the Memorias show no cotton export duty. The only trace is a small tax "Sobre el Algodón" recorded under consumption taxes: ₡0.29M (1956), ₡0.35M (1957), ₡0.03M (1958), then nothing. The second export crop of the 1950s appears to have been almost untaxed at the border. **To verify in the Diario Oficial**, and in the 1960s–70s statements, where a cotton levy could sit in "other merchandise".

#### 4.4 Consumption taxes: how they evolve

- **1950s:** alcohol (liquor and beer), cigarettes, sugar for domestic consumption, soft drinks; cotton (1956–58).
- **1963:** new taxes on petroleum products refined in the country and on flour. Petroleum became the second consumption tax by the late 1960s.
- **1975–79:** the statements add a **selective consumption tax** (*Impuesto Selectivo al Consumo*, ₡9.2M in 1975, ₡22.7M in 1977) and a tax on matches (introduced in 1959).

**Proposed lines:** alcohol; cigarettes; petroleum products; sugar; beverages; selective consumption tax; flour, matches and other.

#### 4.5 Stamps and services

- **Stamp duties** (*timbres fiscales* and sealed paper) are separated. They are the largest piece of this group: ₡87.8M of stamps in 1979, and they doubled in 1971.
- **Services and other taxes:**
  - taxes on the use of services (vehicle registration, air and sea passages, insurance premiums, public shows);
  - business licences and levies;
  - other taxes (migration, etc.).

### 5. What the detailed series shows

1. **The 1960s reform was a turn to consumption and income, not to wealth.** Under Rivera, income tax rose from 8–10% to 15.5% of revenue and the new petroleum and flour taxes (1963) added about 5 points. The net wealth tax stayed at about 2%: the base the elite held in land and capital was barely touched.
2. **The wealth tax moved twice.** Its share tripled under Sánchez Hernández (₡5.7M in 1967 to ₡15.4M in 1968, before the war) and its yield doubled again in 1977 (₡25.9M to ₡54.0M), at the peak of the coffee boom. The 1977 increase is not yet explained; it is now in the Diario Oficial checklist.
3. **Corporate income tax never passed 7% of revenue.** Personal income tax was larger in every period with data. Corporate income tax was 13–61% of the coffee duty (Charts_Detail).
4. **Alcohol and cigarettes carried a sixth of the budget until the late 1960s** (14–17% of revenue), falling to about 10% in the 1970s. Until 1961 these two excises yielded more than all direct taxes together.
5. **Cotton was not taxed at the border.** The only trace is a small tax "Sobre el Algodón" in 1956–58 (₡0.29M at most). The 1975 sugar export tax (₡25.6M) was the only other agro-export levy of weight, and it yielded mainly in that one year (₡3.3M in 1976).
6. **"Other merchandise" export duties are the puzzle of 1977–78** (₡41.9M and ₡116.6M, 5.8% of revenue under Romero). Their legal basis is still to be identified.
7. **Stamps grew into a mass tax.** Stamp duties rose from ₡2.8M (1.6% of revenue) in 1956 to ₡87.8M (7.2%) in 1979; they doubled in 1971.
8. **The agricultural land tax yielded ₡10.1M in 1978 and ₡7.7M in 1979**, about 1% of revenue: the only tax on land of the period, decreed in December 1977, a year after the 1976 agrarian transformation was withdrawn.

### 7. The Sánchez Hernández period (author's decision, 12 November 2026)

#### 7.1 The decision and the author's reasoning

Sánchez Hernández (1967–1972) stays a period of its own. The author reads it as the **transition** between the modernization project with political opening (Osorio to Rivera) and the most authoritarian administration, General Romero's:

- the war with Honduras (1969) unravels the import-substitution (ISI) experiment built on the Common Market;
- coffee becomes less stable;
- there is no programme to resettle the Salvadorans expelled from Honduras (recorded in the annual notes);
- the opposition grows and the first guerrilla groups form;
- the fiscal cost of the war is paid with coffee windfalls; its economic cost is significant but has not been estimated;
- the 1972 electoral fraud is the official end of the political opening of the early 1960s, and Molina closes the phase with the military takeover of the National University.

The author also suggests that the **tax expenditures of the Molina and Romero years** may have been designed to compensate the firms of the incipient ISI sector.

#### 7.2 What the series says about this reading

**Coffee prices.** The data support "less stable than under Rivera", but not yet wild swings: those come in 1976–78.

| Annual change in the coffee price (Pink Sheet) | Largest rise | Largest fall |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Rivera 1962–67 | +25% (1964) | −7% (1962, 1966) |
| Sánchez Hernández 1968–72 | +31% (1970) | −13% (1971) |
| Molina 1973–77 | +118% (1976) | −1% (1975) |
| Romero 1978–79 | +7% (1979) | −31% (1978) |

**The 1970 windfall.** The price rise of 1970 raised the coffee duty by 63% (from ₡26.5M to ₡43.1M). Together with the 1969 emergency tax, that is the money that paid for the war: the annual notes show that the war was financed with war bonds (₡34.3M sold in 1968–70) and the emergency tax (₡9.6M), and that the 1970 windfall wiped out the 1969 financial deficit and repaid ₡10M to the Central Bank (Note_MH_1970). **This fits the author's reading.**

**Import duties.** They had already fallen before the war: ₡59.4M in 1967, ₡52.9M in 1968. Their share of current revenue went from 26% (1967) to 23% in 1968–71 and 22% in 1972. **The war does not show up as a break in import duties.** The reason is probably structural: trade with Honduras and the rest of the Common Market was mostly duty-free, so the war cut trade that paid little tax anyway. The 1968 fall comes first and needs its own explanation. A candidate is the 1968 San José Protocol of the Common Market (emergency measures for the balance of payments); this is to verify, not assumed.

#### 7.3 Estimating the loss of taxable imports: a proposal

Three steps, from the simplest:

1. **Counterfactual import duties.** Project the duties of 1969–72 with the 1960–67 relation between import duties and imports (or GDP), and compare with the actual figures. This needs annual imports by origin (BCR, *Revista Mensual*; SIECA) and the effective duty rate.
2. **Split by origin.** Separate extra-regional imports (taxable) from Central American imports (duty-free). Only the first carry fiscal loss. The economic loss lies more in **exports** to Honduras and in transit across Honduras to Nicaragua and Costa Rica, closed from 1969.
3. **Exemptions.** Industrial-promotion exemptions on imported inputs (Ley de Fomento Industrial) cut the tax base further. The Memorias do not report them, so the size of the taxable base after exemptions needs another source (CEPAL, World Bank 1974).

**Expected result.** The direct loss of import duties is probably small. The large cost of the war is economic (exports, industrial employment, the returnees), not fiscal at the border. That itself supports the author's point that the war's economic cost was never accounted for.

#### 7.4 Tax expenditures as compensation (Molina and Romero): what is needed

The hypothesis is worth testing, but the Memorias do not measure tax expenditures. We need:

- **The laws:** the export-promotion and industrial-promotion laws of the 1970s, and any free zones, with dates and beneficiaries (Diario Oficial). These are added to the checklist; none is assumed here.
- **A size:** registered exemptions by firm or sector (Ministerio de Economía; World Bank 1979 report).
- **The link to ISI firms:** whether the beneficiaries were the firms hit by the loss of the Honduran market.

#### 7.5 A timing point for the periods (corrected 12 November 2026)

The 1972 events split across the change of government:

- **Under Sánchez Hernández:** the fraudulent presidential election of 20 February 1972, which made Molina president, the contested legislative and municipal elections of 12 March, and the failed coup of 25 March.
- **Under Molina:** he took office on 1 July 1972. The military takeover of the National University followed on 19 July 1972, three weeks later.

Our rule assigns the fiscal year 1972 to Sánchez Hernández: its budget was enacted in December 1971, and he governed the first half of the year. The fraud is therefore inside his period, as the author's reading requires. The University takeover is Molina's act, although it happened in the 1972 fiscal year. It opens his period politically; in the revenue series, his period starts with the 1973 budget.

![Spending shares](/home/claude/el-salvador-s-political-economy-of-fiscal-policy-1953-1980/Fiscal Accounts 1952-1979/charts/spending_shares.png){width=6.5in}

- **The windfalls were not spent on security.** In boom years (with a comparable basis), the extra spending went:
  - 44% to development;
  - 20% to education;
  - 14% to security.
  - This is close to average shares. The coffee money of the 1970s financed the expansion of the civilian State.
- **Education peaked in 1971 (27.4% of spending),** the year of the second ANDES strike. It declined in almost every year after, to 20.1% in 1979. This is consistent with H1: education was squeezed in relative terms from the moment the teachers' union became a leading organized opposition.
- **Security fell to 6.5% of spending in 1976 and doubled to 12.7% in 1979.** In money it went from ₡93.7M (1977) to ₡185.0M (1979).
  - In the 1978 bust, security rose ₡29.7M while education fell ₡2.1M.
  - This is the largest such shift in the series; the only precedent, 1958, was tiny (security +₡0.2M, education −₡1.2M). It is the Romero turn, and it shows up clearly in the accounts.
- **Agriculture tripled its share between the 1950s and Molina (9.5%),** including the agrarian programmes and the ISTA (created in 1975).

#### 2.4 Economic classification: what the available years show

- **Wages** were 48% of gastos in the 1950s and 41% in 1979. Their weight fell as transfers and investment grew.
- **Real investment** was 5% of gastos in 1952, 16% in 1954, 11–17% in 1970–71 and 11–14% in 1976–79.
- **In the 1978 bust,** current transfers rose 46% and wages 18%, while real investment fell 3%. Investment again absorbed the adjustment (H3).
- **Capital transfers** to the public sector (autonomous institutions) became large in 1976–79: ₡100–131M a year, 11–14% of gastos.

---

### 3. Reading against the thesis

1. **The regime did not stabilize the coffee cycle; it transmitted it.** Spending rose with every boom and was not cut in the busts of the 1970s. This made the State's growth dependent on coffee prices, as the revenue series already suggested.
2. **The response to busts changed from cutting (1950s–60s) to borrowing (1970s).** That postponed conflict over who would bear the adjustment, at the cost of debt. By 1978–79 the room was gone.
3. **The coffee windfalls built the civilian State, not the repressive one,** until 1977. The security turn of 1978–79 came with a bust, not a boom. When money became scarce, security was protected and education was not.
4. **The education squeeze starts in 1971, not 1978,** and coincides with the teachers' mobilization. This needs the unit lines (teachers' salaries, University subsidy) to confirm (next step).

These are descriptive results from about 26 annual changes. They show timing and direction; they do not establish causation.

| Line | Source |
|:--|:--|
| Current revenue (tax and non-tax, by source) | Revenue_CM and Revenue_Detail (existing) |
| Primary spending: wages; goods and services; transfers; capital | Exp_Economic |

**Why interest deserves its own line:**

- **Primary against overall balance.** This separates what each government decided from what it inherited.
- **The cost of borrowing.** The implicit interest rate shows how this cost changed as lending shifted: from the IBRD, the IDB and AID, to commercial borrowing and domestic bonds.
- **The framework and the stress tests** in section 3 run on the debt dynamics: interest = rate × debt.
- **Interest against spending.** Interest can be set against education or security spending. That makes it a political-economy indicator in its own right: who was paid first in a bust.

### 2. Econometric analysis of fiscal policy

#### 2.1 The constraint

We have **about 27 annual observations (1953–79)**, with structural breaks (1961–63 tax reforms, 1969 war, 1976–78 boom) and some measurement gaps. That rules out the standard large models (e.g., a structural VAR of the Blanchard–Perotti type) as a credible stand-alone exercise. It still allows well-chosen, transparent tests. **El Salvador has one advantage: an exogenous shock that is measured well**, the world coffee price. It works as a natural experiment.

#### 2.2 Options, from the most robust to the most ambitious

1. **Cyclicality of fiscal policy (descriptive and simple regressions).**
   - Regress the change in real spending (total and by component) on the change in output, or directly on the coffee price or coffee duty.
   - Test **asymmetry**: do booms raise spending more than busts cut it? This is the "ratchet" and "when it rains, it pours" literature on Latin America (Gavin and Perotti, 1997; Kaminsky, Reinhart and Végh, 2004).
   - This answers a core question of the project: did the regime spend windfalls and cut in busts?
2. **Where the windfalls went.** Regress each component's change on the change in coffee revenue:
   - security; education; University; investment; wages; debt repayment.
   - The coefficients say what share of each extra colón of coffee revenue went to each use. This is the cleanest quantitative test of H1–H5.
3. **Event studies around the shocks:** the 1958–59 bust, the 1969 war, the 1970 windfall, the 1976–77 boom and the 1978 bust. Each component's path from two years before to two years after, against its trend. This is transparent and suited to policy readers.
4. **Revenue and spending dynamics.** Test whether revenue drives spending or the reverse ("tax-and-spend" against "spend-and-tax"), with an error-correction model. It checks whether the regime adjusted spending to revenue or ran deficits and financed them.
5. **Impact on growth (the fiscal multiplier).** Use local projections (Jordà, 2005), with the coffee price shock as an instrument for revenue and spending, and output as the outcome. This is the most ambitious option and the least precise with 27 observations.
   - **A way to gain power:** a **Central American panel** (Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica; all coffee exporters, with similar regimes) from CEPAL and IMF data. It turns the national story into a comparative test and gives about five times the observations.
6. **The link to polarization.** Relate fiscal variables (education spending per student, teachers' real wages, security share) to measures of mobilization and repression: strikes, protest events, political killings. The best-known event data are in Almeida, *Waves of Protest: Popular Struggle in El Salvador, 1925–2005* (2008). This can show timing and association, **not causation**; the note would say so.

### 3. A retrospective medium-term fiscal framework and stress tests

#### 3.1 The idea

Build a simple MTFF of the kind a finance ministry would use today, and run it **as if prepared at key dates** with the information then available (for example 1962, 1968, 1973, 1977). Then shock it. It answers: how exposed was the State, what would a prudent rule have required, and what did the regime actually do?

#### 3.2 A model small enough to calibrate

- **Revenue:**
  - coffee duty = price × volume × the duty schedule;
  - other taxes = elasticity × GDP growth (elasticities estimated from the series);
  - non-tax revenue as a share.
- **Spending:**
  - wages and transfers with **inertia** (the rigid part);
  - investment as the **adjustment variable**, as observed;
  - interest = average rate × debt.
- **Financing:**
  - external loans (by lender, from the loan register);
  - domestic bonds;
  - Central Bank credit.
- **Outputs:**
  - balance;
  - debt path;
  - gross financing needs;
  - the adjustment required to keep debt or the deficit within a limit.

#### 3.3 Stress tests (historical, so they are realistic)

| Shock | Calibration |
|:--|:--|
| Coffee price fall | −30% (1978), −13% (1971), and a bootstrapped distribution of 1950–79 price changes for fan charts |
| Coffee volume shock (frost, rust, labour) | One bad harvest |
| Loss of the Common Market | The 1969 war: exports and transit to Honduras |
| War or security spending shock | The 1968–70 war costs |
| Interest-rate and external-credit shock | 1979–80 |
| Combined | Coffee bust with an interest-rate shock (1978–80) |

#### 3.4 Policy counterfactuals the framework allows

- **A coffee stabilization fund:** save the duty above a reference price in 1976–77, and spend it in 1978–79. How much of the 1978 deficit would it have covered?
- **A spending rule:** for example, current spending growing with trend revenue, not with the coffee duty.
- **Protecting development spending:** what the 1978 bust would have required if education and health had been protected.

These follow the logic of the modern fiscal-risk tools (IMF, *Analyzing and Managing Fiscal Risks: Best Practices*, 2016; the IMF debt sustainability framework). They are applied with modest, transparent calibration.

# Part 6. Validating the Three Pillars: What to Check, Where, and How


**Working note for discussion — 12 November 2026.** The author's point: the three pillars (development, security, agro-export support) are built from ministry totals in the Memorias. Before they carry the argument of the paper, they need validation with micro data from the Diario Oficial and other sources. This note sets out where the current measure can mislead, the tests that would validate or correct it, the sources, and a sequence.

---

### 1. How the pillars are measured now, and why that is not enough

**Current measure.** Ministry (*ramo*) totals from the Memorias, on a credits-used basis where available:

- **Development** = Educación + Salud Pública + Obras Públicas;
- **Security** = Defensa + Seguridad Pública;
- **Agro-export support** = Agricultura y Ganadería.

**Where it can mislead:**

| Risk | Example | Direction of error |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Security spending outside Defensa and Seguridad Pública | Police or paramilitary units in Interior; the rural network ORDEN; Presidency lump sums (*partidas* for "security" or "special expenses"); military pensions in Clases Pasivas; war-bond service in Deuda Pública | Security understated |
| Security inside other lines of Defensa | Guardia Nacional, Policía de Hacienda and Policía Nacional moved between Defensa and Seguridad Pública across years | Shifts within the pillar; breaks in the series |
| Spending outside the ordinary budget | Extraordinary budgets and special decrees (*créditos extraordinarios*, *refuerzos*); arms purchases financed by loans or special bonds (1969); military aid in kind | Security understated, especially in crisis years |
| Development lines that serve the coalition | Roads to coffee and cotton zones; the port of Acajutla; electricity for industry | Development overstated as "legitimacy"; agro-export support understated |
| Agro-export support through the tax system and credit, not the budget | The coffee income-tax exemption (1950); untaxed cotton; credit to the coffee sector (the ₡30M General Fund loan to the Compañía Salvadoreña de Café, 1975) | Agro-export support badly understated |
| Autonomous institutions off-budget | CEL, IRA, ISTA, ISSS, the University (only the subsidy is in the budget) | All pillars understated; agrarian and food programmes especially |
| Basis changes | Credits used against gastos against final execution | Artificial jumps (marked in the workbook) |
| Nominal shares | Shares can rise while real spending per teacher or per student falls | Development's "legitimacy" overstated in inflationary years (1974–79) |

The 1950s results (security 13–16%, development 38–46%) are probably robust in direction. The 1970s results, especially the security doubling of 1978–79 and the agro-export overtaking of 1974–77, are the ones most exposed to these errors.

# Part 7. Balancing the Pillars: The Coalition Pillar, in Spending and in Tax Spending


**Working note for discussion — 12 November 2026.** It records the author's decisions on the validation of the three pillars and sets out how to build the third pillar as a **coalition pillar**: everything the State gave the coffee, agro-export and industrial coalition, through the budget, the tax system or credit. It includes a first, partial estimate that shows why the change matters.

---

### 1. Decisions recorded (12 November 2026)

| Question | Decision |
|:--|:--|
| Balance of the pillars | The third pillar becomes a **coalition pillar**, combining spending and tax spending (tax expenditures), and credit |
| Diario Oficial | The author has access to all issues |
| Benchmark years | 1954, 1959, 1965, 1969, 1971, 1976, 1978, 1979 |
| Interior (Gobernación) | Reviewed unit by unit for the 1970s (ORDEN; rural network) |
| Agro-export support | Broadened to include tax breaks and credit |

---

### 2. Why the balance needs the tax side

A budget-only comparison is lopsided:

- **Development and security are delivered mainly through spending.** The pillar measures capture most of them.
- **The coalition was served mainly through the tax system and credit.** The main instruments:
  - the coffee income-tax exemption (1950);
  - cotton's freedom from export duty;
  - the duty-free imports and income-tax holidays of industrial promotion (1952, 1961, the Central American Agreement on Fiscal Incentives ratified in 1969);
  - export promotion and free zones (1970, 1974);
  - bank credit: coffee and cotton were about 80% of the banks' agricultural portfolio in the early 1970s (World Bank, 1974).
- **None of these appears in the budget.**

Comparing a spending-only coalition pillar with a security pillar therefore understates the coalition by design. The measure of the bargain should put all three pillars on the same footing: **resources the State committed to each group, whether spent or forgone.**

---

### 3. The coalition pillar: components

| Component | Instrument | Measure | Source |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **A. Budget** | Agricultura y Ganadería units serving export crops (coffee and cotton research, extension, credit, plant health); export-promotion bodies | Credits used by unit | Memorias, unit tables (Estado N° 5, IV-2); Ley de Presupuesto and Ley de Salarios (DO) |
| **B. Tax spending** | (1) Coffee income-tax exemption (D. 836, 1950) | Coffee profits × applicable income-tax rate, less the export duty treated as a substitute (two variants) | DO (law and any amendments); coffee value added and costs (BCR; CEPAL; World Bank) |
| | (2) Untaxed cotton (and sugar before November 1974) | Cotton exports × a counterfactual duty rate (range) | Export values (CEPAL; World Bank; BCR); duty laws (DO) |
| | (3) Industrial promotion: import-duty and income-tax exemptions | Exempted imports × tariff; exempted profits × rate | **DO: the executive agreements (*acuerdos*) that classified each firm under the promotion laws**; Ministry of Economy; World Bank (1979) |
| | (4) Export promotion and free zones (1970, 1974) | Same method | DO; World Bank (1979) |
| **C. Credit and guarantees** | General Fund loans and State guarantees to the coffee sector (e.g. ₡30M to the Compañía Salvadoreña de Café, 1975); BCR rediscount lines for coffee and cotton | New lending each year; the implicit subsidy (rate gap) where terms are known | Memorias (General Fund balance sheet; guarantees); BCR reports; DO (loan decrees) |
| **Memo (not in the pillar)** | Public works serving export zones (coffee and cotton roads, the Litoral highway, Acajutla) | Shown separately, so development is not overstated | Loan register; extraordinary budgets |

**The other two pillars, validated in the same way:**

- **Security:**
  - all armed and police units wherever they sit, including Interior and the Presidency;
  - ORDEN;
  - extraordinary credits to Defence;
  - the 1969 war bonds;
  - military aid (memo).
- **Development:** Education, Health and Public Works. Public works serving export zones moves to the memo line.

**The common denominator.** Shares of **total public effort**: credits used plus the coalition's tax spending plus net new credit. Each pillar is then shown as a share of that total, so that a colón forgone counts the same as a colón spent.

### 4. A first, partial estimate: untaxed cotton

Only one component can be estimated now from data in hand. It shows the order of magnitude.

**Method.** Cotton exports times a counterfactual duty:

- **Upper bound:** the effective rate actually paid by coffee that year (coffee duty / coffee export value).
- **Lower bound:** a flat 5%, a modest export levy of the kind the government considered for cotton in 1973–74. The World Bank's 1974 memorandum reports that the authorities were "considering … export duties on cotton and sugar which will vary on a progressive basis". Sugar was taxed from November 1974; cotton was not.

**What the partial estimate already shows:**

1. **Cotton alone is worth 1–6% of revenue** in forgone duty. That is comparable to the whole Agriculture budget in the 1950s (₡5.2M in 1957).
2. **The coalition pillar reaches the size of the security pillar by 1965,** even before the two largest items are counted: the coffee income-tax exemption and the industrial exemptions.
3. **The 1950s reading changes.** On the budget alone, the coalition got 3%. With one tax item added, it got 4–7%. With the coffee exemption, it is likely to be larger still. This supports the hypothesis that the coalition was served mainly through the tax system.
4. **The 1970s turning points must be re-read.** Agriculture "overtaking" security in 1974–77 partly reflects the agrarian programmes, which are not coalition spending. After the unit review, the coalition pillar may be smaller in the budget and larger in tax spending.

# Part 8. Unit-Level Review of the Pillars: Pilot Year 1977


**Working note for discussion — 12 November 2026.** This note is the first step of the validation agreed today. It reviews Interior, the Presidency and Agriculture unit by unit, plus the coalition items found in other ministries, for one pilot year. It then rebuilds the three pillars for that year with the coalition pillar balanced (spending plus tax spending).

**Pilot year: 1977.** That year's units are reported in Estado IV-2 of the 1978 Memoria (*Estado de gastos con especificación de unidades de organización y clases generales del Presupuesto General de 1977*). The 1977 budget was executed half under Molina and half under Romero, who took office on 1 July. Every figure below was read on the page image.

**Basis.** Estado IV-2 records budget execution by unit. Its grand total for 1977 is ₡1,010.54M, which differs from the credits used of the fiscal workbook (₡1,241.9M). Shares in this note are of the IV-2 total, so they are internally consistent but not identical to the workbook's pillar shares.

---

### 1. Interior: no police, one coalition item

Interior spent ₡33.80M in 1977. Its units are civil:

- central administration;
- regional development and governors' offices;
- the national printing office and the Diario Oficial;
- the postal service;
- communications operations;
- migration control, which includes an "Investigaciones" sub-unit of ₡0.14M;
- transfers to municipalities;
- the construction of the ministry's building (₡4.23M).

**No police, paramilitary or ORDEN unit appears in Interior in 1977.** The security apparatus is not hidden in this ministry's budget.

**A coalition item.** Interior paid **₡6.96M "to the municipalities of the Republic to compensate for the suppression of taxes on the cultivation of coffee"**. Municipal taxes on coffee had been abolished, and the central government replaced the lost revenue from the general budget. In effect, all taxpayers paid the coffee growers' former local taxes. The 1950 coffee decree (D. 836) already assigned part of the coffee tax to coffee municipalities; the date of the suppression and of this compensation is to be checked in the Diario Oficial.

### 6. What the pilot shows

1. **Security is not hidden in Interior.** In 1977 the police were in Seguridad Pública and Defensa, as assumed. The security pillar holds, but the Presidency's ₡19.4M of opaque lump sums is the main uncertainty. If even half of it served security, the security pillar would rise from 8.3% to about 9.3%.
2. **The coalition is served on the tax side.** On the budget, the coalition received 1.7% of spending in 1977, much less than the provisional "Agriculture" pillar suggested. With untaxed cotton alone, it reaches 2.7–6.8% of total effort. The coffee and industrial exemptions are still missing, so the true figure is higher.
3. **Some coalition spending is hidden in other ministries.** The best example is Interior's ₡6.96M to replace the municipal coffee taxes, a transfer from general taxpayers to the coffee municipalities, booked as municipal finance.
4. **The "agro-export overtakes security" result of 1974–77 does not survive as stated.** It came from counting all of Agriculture, including IRA and ISTA. On a unit basis, the coalition's budget share is small. Its weight lies in tax spending.
