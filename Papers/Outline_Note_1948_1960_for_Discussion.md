## 2. Purpose of the 1948–1960 note

This note covers the founding decade of the modernization project, from Osorio's coup (December 1948) to the fall of Lemus (October 1960). It asks three questions:

1. **The starting point.** What were the economy and the State at the start? (A simple, rural agro-export economy.)
2. **The project.** What did the reforms try to change (diversification, industry, a technical cadre, a middle class), and how were they financed?
3. **What was building up.** Which forces were born or strengthened under the apparent stability of the decade, to become decisive in the 1960s and 1970s?

It builds on what exists:

- the yearly notes 1952–1960;
- the Narrative Exercise 1952–1960, which tested seven claims;
- the revenue and fiscal workbooks;
- Turcios, Pleitez and Dada Hirezi;
- the literature in the reading list.

## 3. Proposed structure (about 15–20 pages)

### 1. Introduction

- The decade in the project: the author's reading is that until Rivera the country is guided by the modernization begun by Osorio. Its stability was partly superficial: forces were born and built up that became decisive in the 1970s.
- What the note adds:
  - the fiscal record, verified year by year;
  - the international setting;
  - a first look at diversification ("complexity").

### 2. The point of departure, c. 1948

- **The economy:**
  - coffee as the economy;
  - land and labour after 1932;
  - the Martínez regime's restrictions on industry (Dada Hirezi: the 1939 ban on factories above ₡20,000 in some lines);
  - the cotton beginnings.
- **The State:** a small budget financed by coffee and import duties; the fixed parity of ₡2.50 per dollar since 1935.
- **A benchmark:** Wallich and Adler's study of Salvadoran public finance (1949), written just as the decade began. It is worth obtaining as the "before" picture, together with Pleitez's 1948 shares:
  - export taxes 14.3% of tax revenue;
  - income tax 5.7%.

### 3. The international setting

**3.1 Development thought.**

- ECLA and Prebisch (1949–50): the terms-of-trade argument, industrialization and integration.
- The Hoselitz mission (1954), "the bible" of Lemus's economic policy (Pleitez).
- ECLA's Committee for Economic Cooperation in Central America (from 1951), and the integration treaties of 1951–1960.

**3.2 The coffee regime.** The author's point about oligopoly pricing needs one qualification, to be checked and written carefully.

- **1940–1948:** the Inter-American Coffee Agreement (1940), backed by the United States, set export quotas to the US market for the Latin American producers. Prices were then held by wartime price controls in the United States. The coffee producers were given an assured market at a time when European markets were closed.
- **After the war,** prices rose to a peak in the mid-1950s. The 1950s boom appears to have been driven mainly by the market (post-war demand and supply problems in Brazil), not by a working cartel. The producers' attempts to support prices came as prices fell:
  - a "gentleman's agreement" on minimum prices (1954);
  - the Mexico Agreement (1957);
  - the Latin American Coffee Agreement (1958);
  - the first short-term International Coffee Agreement (1959/60, with African producers).
- **The effective cartel came in the 1960s.** The 1962 International Coffee Agreement, with the United States as a member after Kennedy's Alliance for Progress shift, had export quotas for all members and entered into force in 1963.
- **For this note:**
  - El Salvador's 1950s windfall was a market windfall;
  - the 1958–59 fall was the market correcting;
  - the price support that benefited small producers belongs mainly to the 1960s (Rivera), which fits the author's view of that period.
- **To verify:**
  - the US premium for agricultural commodities in the early 1950s (the Korean War years; any US price ceilings on coffee in 1951);
  - El Salvador's role in the regional agreements (FEDECAME, founded by Roberto Canessa).

**3.3 The United States and the region.**

- US policy towards Central America in the 1950s: the Guatemala intervention of 1954 as the regional signal.
- The late, decisive US support for the form of integration that prevailed (Dada Hirezi).

### 4. The modernization project, 1948–1960

- **The 1950 Constitution:** the social and economic role of the State.
- **Institutions:**
  - CEL (electricity) and the Lempa dam;
  - social security (ISSS);
  - housing (IVU);
  - industrial credit;
  - the technical cadre around the Ministries of Economy and Finance.
  - Names and dates to be checked against Pleitez and Turcios.
- **The fiscal reforms:**
  - the progressive coffee export tax with the income-tax exemption for coffee (D. 836, 1950);
  - the income tax (D. 510, 1951);
  - industrial promotion and its exemptions (D.L. 661, 1952);
  - the net wealth tax (Ley de Vialidad, D.L. 1308, 1953);
  - the 1959 consumption taxes.
- **Spending priorities:**
  - education, health and public works;
  - the large works financed by foreign loans (5 de Noviembre dam, Litoral highway, Acajutla port).

### 5. The fiscal record, 1952–1960 (from the workbooks)

- **Revenue:**
  - border taxes about 60% of revenue;
  - coffee 27% under Osorio and 21% under Lemus;
  - income tax 7–8%;
  - alcohol and cigarettes more than all direct taxes;
  - cotton almost untaxed at the border.
- **Spending:**
  - development rose from 38–39% to 43–45%;
  - security 13–16%, drifting down;
  - education from 14% to 19%.
- **The buffer:** the Superávit Financiero reached ₡45.7M in 1957 and absorbed the 1958 fall.
- **The 1958–59 adjustment:**
  - new consumption taxes;
  - cuts in public works and Defence;
  - education protected;
  - no new borrowing.
- **Debt:** 15–27% of revenue, interest about 1% of revenue.
- **Charts:**
  - revenue composition, 1952–60, with Osorio and Lemus shaded;
  - spending shares;
  - the underlying result and the surplus;
  - the coffee price and the coffee duty, if the 1950s price series is found.

### 6. Diversification and complexity

- **The idea:** Hausmann, Hidalgo and co-authors argue that development is the accumulation of productive capabilities, visible in the variety and sophistication of what a country exports:
  - Hausmann, Hwang and Rodrik (2007), "What You Export Matters";
  - Hidalgo and Hausmann (2009), "The Building Blocks of Economic Complexity";
  - *The Atlas of Economic Complexity* (2011).
- **The question for the 1950s:** did the reforms diversify the economy? A first, simple measure, if the data allow:
  - export concentration (coffee's share; a Herfindahl index of exports, 1948–1960);
  - the rise of cotton, from 1% to 11% of exports (Pleitez);
  - the industrial share of output and employment.
- **A caution:** the formal complexity indices start in the 1960s and need detailed trade data. For the 1950s this section can only be descriptive.

### 7. Forces building beneath the stability

- The new middle groups: State employees, teachers, the University, professionals and the technical cadre.
- Labour: urban unions under government control; the rural minimum-wage bill resisted by landowners in 1960.
- Politics: the PRUD's internal split; the opposition fronts; the University and students; repression in 1960; Cuba's influence; the October 1960 coup.
- **The bridge to the 1960s:** these groups gained voice under the 1961–67 opening and became the core of the opposition in the 1970s.

### 8. Reading the decade against the thesis

This section updates the Narrative Exercise verdicts with the revenue detail and the fiscal workbook:

- coffee financed modernization;
- revenue was exposed to coffee;
- security was not protected in the 1950s;
- the 1958–59 adjustment fell on public works;
- the 1960 crisis was political, not fiscal;
- the tax bargain favoured coffee and capital.

**Proposition for the paper:** the 1950s built the expectations (a developmental State, a middle class, education) and the fiscal model (coffee, trade and a light tax on capital). The conflict of the 1970s came from the collision between the two.
