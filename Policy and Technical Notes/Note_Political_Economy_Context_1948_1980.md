# El Salvador's Political Economy, 1948–1980: Context for the Fiscal Notes

**Working Note — Structural and political context drawn from Pleitez, Dada Hirezi and the conflict literature (November 2026)**

> **About this note.** The yearly fiscal notes have relied mainly on Turcios for political context. Turcios is strong on the 1950s and on the 1960 crisis, but he does not cover the structure of the development model or the 1970s. This note adds two economic histories that are in the repository:
>
> - William Pleitez (chapters 1–2; the repository folder spells it "Pleites"), on the agro-export model and the import-substitution model. Earlier notes cited him only for individual decrees;
> - Héctor Dada Hirezi (1978), on the economy and Central American integration, 1945–1960.
>
> It also adds the IMF growth study in the repository and the main works on the origins of the civil war, consulted through reviews and reference works. It sets out what each source contributes, the fiscal mechanisms these sources point to, and a chronology the yearly notes can draw on. Where the sources give figures, the figures are theirs. They are not yet reconciled with the Memorias de Hacienda, except where stated.

---

## 1. Why Turcios is not enough

Turcios (*Autoritarismo y modernización, 1950–1960*; *Rebelión: San Salvador 1960*) explains the politics of the Osorio and Lemus years and of the 1960 crisis well. His period terms, "democratic transition" and "authoritarian reformism", remain the frame of these notes. But the project's question is about mechanisms. How did the regimes' fiscal and monetary management of the coffee cycle feed polarization? That needs three things Turcios does not give:

1. **The economic model and who gained from it.** Pleitez and Dada Hirezi cover this.
2. **The fiscal bargain written into tax law** (who paid, and who was exempted). Pleitez lists the decrees; the 1961 and 1962 notes used some of them, but not as a system.
3. **The 1960s–1970s sequence:** opening, mobilization, closure and repression. The literature on the origins of the civil war covers this (Stanley, Almeida, Lindo-Fuentes and Ching, Durham, Baloyra).

## 2. The development model and the coalition behind it

### 2.1 Pleitez: from the agro-export model to import substitution

Pleitez calls the post-1948 project the *modelo de industrialización por sustitución de importaciones* (MISI). It replaced the *modelo agroexportador* (MAE). Three factors made the shift possible after the December 1948 coup:

- **political instability**, 1940–48;
- **the cotton boom.** Cotton exports rose 55-fold between 1945 and 1957, from 1% to 11% of exports;
- **CEPAL structuralism.** The Hoselitz report (1954) was, in President Lemus's words, "the bible" of his economic policy.

The 1950 Constitution gave the State a mandate for social justice and development (arts. 2, 135–137, 182).

Pleitez identifies **ten strategies**:

1. new leadership and a coalition;
2. the fiscal space for a developmental State;
3. tax incentives for infant industries;
4. preferential credit;
5. the Ilopango–Soyapango industrial pole and infrastructure;
6. protection and Central American integration;
7. social policy: housing, education and health;
8. social protection and minimum wages;
9. agricultural modernization;
10. macroeconomic stability.

**The coalition.** It had three parts:

- **Military officers:** Osorio, Lemus, then Rivera, Sánchez Hernández, Molina and Romero.
- **Technocrats:**
  - Galindo Pohl (Education, 1950–56);
  - Humberto Costa (Finance under Lemus);
  - Jorge Sol Castellanos (Economy, "the soul of integration");
  - Alfonso Rochac (Economy under Lemus and Rivera; founder of the ABC in 1962 and the BFA in 1973);
  - Enrique Porras (Finance, 1950–54).
- **Businessmen in office:**
  - Roberto Canessa (Foreign Affairs, 1950–54, founder of FEDECAME);
  - Quiñónez Schlesinger and García Prieto (Agriculture);
  - Meza Ayau (Economy);
  - Víctor de Sola (president of CEL, 1950–78).

Pleitez's reading is that the coalition delivered growth, diversification and better public finances, but not inclusion (section 5).

### 2.2 Dada Hirezi: whose model?

Dada Hirezi's book was written in Louvain in 1977 and published by UCA Editores in 1978. It asks who the model served. His argument has four steps:

- **After 1932, employment was a political stabilizer.** After the 1932 massacre the Martínez regime protected artisans and small workshops. It banned machinery for sugar mills and cotton gins. A 1939 decree prohibited factories with capital above ₡20,000 in footwear, metal goods, bricks, soap and similar lines. The aim, he argues, was to prevent the unemployment that could rebuild the urban–rural alliance of 1932. **This is an early instance of the mechanism this project studies: economic policy managed to contain a political threat.**
- **The 1948 regime renewed the hegemonic alliance; it did not break it.** Its measures were:
  - the end of wage payment in kind;
  - repeal of the 1930s anti-industrial restrictions;
  - a higher coffee export tax, to move surplus from the agro-export sector to others;
  - a new income tax;
  - roads, the Lempa dam (power 25% cheaper), and bilateral free-trade treaties;
  - social security and *urban unions under government control*.

  Dada Hirezi calls it industrialization "without breaking the internal social equilibrium."
- **The State's fiscal base was exposed to coffee.** The terms of trade rose from 44.5 (1945) to 147.9 (1954), then fell to 86.8 (1960). After 1954 "the financing of the State could no longer depend so much on the taxes of a sector in difficulty". Current-price GDP fell from ₡1,247.7M (1958) to ₡1,195.4M (1959). This is the economic backdrop of the 1960 crisis that Turcios describes.
- **The Common Market took the form the Salvadoran bourgeoisie wanted.** CEPAL proposed gradual, reciprocal integration with planned location of industry. What prevailed was broad free trade and a high common external tariff. This was the Salvadoran and Guatemalan bilateral model, backed late but decisively by the United States. "The agro-export and import sectors, already associated with foreign capital, were the main beneficiaries of integration in El Salvador, consolidating the existing class structure."

**The Honduras link.** Dada Hirezi stresses that the 1918 free-trade treaty with Honduras let El Salvador send its "surplus population" there. In the 1930s, 40% of the banana workers in Honduras were Salvadoran. He argues that free movement of people within the CACM remained "a means to relieve the pressure of population on the Salvadoran economic structure." This makes the 1969 war the closing of a safety valve that the model had relied on (section 6).

### 2.3 What the two accounts mean for the fiscal notes

Pleitez and Dada Hirezi agree on the facts but differ on the reading:

- For Pleitez, the State gained fiscal space and spent more on people.
- For Dada Hirezi, the State's fiscal role was to move surplus from coffee to industry for the same propertied groups.

**The fiscal notes can test this.** The tests are who paid the taxes, who received the exemptions and how spending responded when coffee fell.

## 3. The fiscal bargain in tax law

Pleitez lists the main tax laws of the period, with their Diario Oficial references. Read together, they show how the burden was shared between coffee, companies and individuals.

**Revenue shares.** According to Pleitez:

- **Export taxes** rose from 14.3% of tax revenue (1948) to an average of 29.8% (1951–57).
- **Income tax** rose from 5.7% (1948) to 20.8% (1978).
- **Averages over the MISI period:**
  - import duties 30.7%;
  - export duties 21.8%;
  - direct taxes 18.6% (income tax 13.5%);
  - liquor 10.1%.

**What this means for the notes.**

1. **The coffee income-tax exemption (D. 836) is a tax expenditure in its own right.** It should enter the tax-expenditure section from 1967 on, alongside the Fomento Industrial exemptions and the CACM tariff losses. It also explains why the coffee export duty, not the income tax, carries the coffee cycle into the budget.
2. **Personal versus company income tax.** The 1951 and 1961 laws were written with a large gap between individuals and companies, and then a reinvestment exemption for industry. This background explains our finding that company income tax fell 19.1% in 1965 and 16.6% in 1966 while personal income tax kept rising.
3. **The Directorio's reforms of 1961 were partly undone within months.** That is a pattern to look for later: reforms announced, then diluted.

Pleitez lists the monetary reforms. Each is a date to watch for in the Treasury's financing.

| 15 Dec 1961 (D. 496) | New *Ley Orgánica* of the BCR; Superintendency of Banks | Mandate: stability and the colón's convertibility |

Pleitez stresses stability: fiscal deficits never exceeded 2% of GDP, and debt reached 18.5% of GDP in 1979. That fits the Memorias so far. **The fixed rate and the small deficits are why adjustment fell on spending composition, not on aggregates.** That is the premise of hypotheses H2 and H3.

## 5. Social spending and its limits

### 5.1 Spending shares

Pleitez's averages for central-government spending:

| Item | MAE (to 1948) | MISI (1948–79) |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Education | 6.1% | 20.2% |
| Health | 4.2% | 10.8% |
| Agriculture | 0.3% | 6.2% |
| Public works | — | 14.6% |
| Defence and security | 31.1% | 11.3% |
| Debt service | 25.6% | 3.6% |

Education peaked at 30.4% of spending in 1971; health peaked at 15.2% in 1968.

**Check against our series.** Education was 22.9% (1966) and 24.4% (1967) of the budget at final execution, and Defence was about 10.4% of 1966 payments. Both fit Pleitez's averages. Pleitez's 1971 education peak and 1968 health peak should be checked when we reach those years.

### 5.2 Education: the 1968 reform and the teachers

The reform was led by Minister Walter Béneke under Sánchez Hernández, 1968–72. It was the largest education effort of the period:

- nine years of basic education;
- closure of the existing teacher-training schools, replaced by the Ciudad Normal Alberto Masferrer;
- educational television;
- diversified *bachilleratos*;
- the Instituto Tecnológico Centroamericano.

Under Molina came "Una escuela por día."

Lindo-Fuentes and Ching (*Modernizing Minds in El Salvador*, 2012; Spanish edition 2017) treat the reform as a Cold War modernization project designed without the teachers. Three points matter here:

- **Teachers turned into the opposition.** Teachers, once among the regime's most loyal employees, became one of its strongest opponents. This happened through ANDES's strikes of 1968 and 1971.
- **The 1971 strike.** It lasted 53 days in June–July 1971. It demanded a new salary scale by grade and seniority, social security and better schools, and it opposed parts of the reform. Decree 390 declared it illegal. The National Guard broke it up; at least two teachers and three students were killed. None of the demands were met (Swarthmore Global Nonviolent Action Database).
- **Rural expansion was slower than urban** (Lindo-Fuentes, cited by Pleitez).

**This is the clearest case so far of the mechanism behind H2.** Spending on education grew, but salary demands were refused and the protest was repressed.

## 6. The distributional outcome and the safety valve

### 6.1 Growth without inclusion (Pleitez)

Pleitez is explicit: under the MISI "there was high growth, [but] income distribution became more unequal," and conflict did not fall.

| Indicator | c. 1950 | c. 1960–61 | c. 1970–71 | c. 1975–80 | Source cited by Pleitez |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Income share, richest 20% | 8% held 51% (1950) | 61.4% | 50.8% | 66.0% (1980) | UN; CEPAL |
| Income share, poorest 20% | — | 5.5% | 3.7% | 2.0% (1980) | CEPAL |
| Landless families, % of rural families | — | 19.8% (1961) | 31.8% (1971) | 41.1% (1975) | Montes, *El agro salvadoreño* |
| Labour under-utilization (under- plus unemployment) | 53.8% | — | 54.8% | 60.2% (1980) | PREALC |
| Employment: agriculture / industry / commerce and services | 64.6 / 12.3 / 21.4 | 60.6 / 13.1 / 23.9 | 54.9 / 11.1 / 31.4 | 41.5 / 14.2 / 40.0 | CEPAL |
| ISSS contributors, % of the labour force | — | — | — | 9.4% (1979) | |

Other findings Pleitez reports:

- **Real minimum wages.** The real agricultural minimum wage lost 14% of its purchasing power between 1965 (when it was created) and 1978. Urban minimum wages lost more than 5%.
- **Productivity rose but TFP fell in the 1970s.** Labour productivity kept rising, but total factor productivity growth turned negative (−1.3% a year).
- **Capital's share.** Sevilla estimates that capital received 56 centavos of each colón of income in 1971–79.
- **Land concentration.** Farms of 200 hectares or more (0.32% of farms) held 28.4% of farmland in 1971.
- **Seasonal labour.** Coffee labour demand rose 204% between May and December.

*Discrepancy to flag.* Pleitez's text gives landless families as 11.8%, 29.1% and 40.9%, but his Table 2.17 gives 19.8%, 31.8% and 41.1%. The table is used above; Montes's original should be checked.

### 6.2 Honduras: the closing of the safety valve

The sources line up on the following points:

- **Dada Hirezi:** emigration to Honduras was structural, written into the 1918 treaty and sought again in the CACM.
- **Durham (*Scarcity and Survival in Central America*, 1979):** the cause was the use and distribution of land, not population as such. "Land is not scarce for large landholders." The migrants were mostly landless renters and sharecroppers pushed out as cotton and cattle expanded.
- **Library of Congress country studies:**
  - about 300,000 Salvadorans were in Honduras by 1969;
  - in April 1969 the Honduran INA announced it would evict non-native beneficiaries of land reform;
  - fighting ran from 14 to 18 July 1969, with up to 2,000 dead, mostly Honduran civilians;
  - 60,000–130,000 Salvadorans were expelled or fled;
  - trade between the two countries stopped and the CACM became "a paper entity";
  - El Salvador "lost the economic safety valve" and had to pay heavily to restock its arsenal.
- **Pleitez** lists the repatriation from Honduras among the causes of insufficient employment in the 1970s. He dates Honduras's exit from integration to after "the armed conflict ... in 1968"; the war was in 1969.

**Fiscal implications to track from 1969.** The events point to four effects on the accounts:

1. defence spending and arms purchases (see *Note_Military_Financing_1967_1969*);
2. any spending on the resettlement of returnees;
3. customs revenue lost with the Honduran market;
4. the export-promotion incentives of 1970 and 1974, as new tax expenditures.

## 7. From opening to closure: the political sequence

### 7.1 Interpretive frames

Three works explain the transition from reform to war:

- **Almeida (*Waves of Protest*, 2008).** El Salvador had two protest waves:
  - The first, in the 1960s, was driven by **institutional access**: proportional representation from 1964, the PDC's municipal gains, legal unions and ANDES.
  - The second, in the 1970s, was driven by **threats**: economic losses, electoral fraud and repression. It pushed organizations toward revolutionary coalitions.

  The timing of state liberalization, and of its reversal, shapes the form protest takes.
- **Stanley (*The Protection Racket State*, 1996).** In Mason's review (*LARR* 1999), the military protected the agro-export elite's dominance in exchange for control of the State. Repression often exceeded what was needed to contain the threat, and it served to justify more resources for the military. The officer corps had institutional interests of its own. **This bears on the "security" pillar.** Growth in Defence and Seguridad Pública is not only a response to threats; it may also be the price of the protection bargain.
- **Baloyra (*El Salvador in Transition*, 1982).** Cited by the Library of Congress, his data show that between 1972 and 1979 political assassinations rose tenfold, prosecutions of "subversives" tripled, and disappearances doubled.

## 8. What this adds to the hypotheses

The five hypotheses stand. The sources suggest four additions to track, not new hypotheses:

| Hypothesis | What the sources add | What to look for in the Memorias |
|:--|:--|:--|
| H1 Education and the University squeezed in downturns | Education grew as a share of spending, to 30.4% in 1971 (Pleitez); conflict arose over *how* (the reform) and *pay*, not only the total | The education wage bill against posts; the University's subsidy around the 1972 intervention |
| H2 Wages contained, especially teachers' | 1971 strike over the salary scale; real minimum wages fell 14% from 1965 to 1978 | Teachers' salary laws (Diario Oficial); the payroll in 1970–72 |
| H3 Public investment cut first | Confirmed in 1966–67; the model relied on loan-financed infrastructure | Continue the capital budget series |
| H4 Monetary tightening | 1961 BCR reforms; 1966 Fondo de Desarrollo Económico; 1973 Junta Monetaria | Treasury use of BCR credit and Treasury notes |
| H5 IRA food subsidies cut | Rising landlessness and seasonal unemployment raised the stakes of food prices | IRA transfers 1968–80 |
| *Track A: the tax bargain* | Coffee exempt from income tax (1950); company preferences; reinvestment exemption; Fomento Industrial; export incentives 1970/74 | Tax-expenditure section; personal versus company income tax |
| *Track B: the safety valve* | Honduras as outlet for landless workers; closed in 1969 | Returnee spending; customs loss; defence 1969–71 |
| *Track C: the protection bargain* | Military protection of the elite in exchange for control of the State (Stanley) | Defence, Seguridad Pública, Casa Presidencial, the *partidas secretas* |
| *Track D: reform announced, then diluted* | D. 300 diluted by D. 442 (1961); the 1974 land law not enforced; 1976 land reform withdrawn | Follow each reform's fiscal trace |

## 9. Cautions

- **Coffee prices and growth.** The IMF study in the repository (Morales, WP/98/104, 1998) covers 1970–1995. It found that neither the terms of trade nor coffee prices had a significant effect on long-run growth. That does not undermine this project. The thesis runs through the **budget and distribution**, not through trend growth. It does mean the notes should not claim that coffee drove the business cycle without showing the fiscal channel. The same study dates the war to 1978–1990 and records the migration of about 40% of the working-age population between 1975 and 1990.
- **Errors in the sources.** Pleitez misdates the Honduras war (1968 for 1969), and his landless figures conflict between text and table. Dada Hirezi's figures come from OCR of a scanned copy; numbers quoted here were checked against the page text, but should be confirmed against the book before publication.
- **The danger of reading history backward.** The 1960s were also a decade of real reform and growth. The sources argue that polarization came from the *combination* of growth without inclusion, the closing of political and migratory outlets, and repression. They do not attribute it to economic failure alone. The fiscal notes should test that combination year by year.

1. **Political Context sections** will carry a short "political economy reading" of the year drawing on these sources. Wikipedia citations will be replaced with the Library of Congress, DADM and the academic works.
