# El Salvador's Fiscal Landscape, 1952–1953: Evidence from the Memorias de Hacienda

**Working Note — Based on Primary Sources (revised November 2026)**

The main corrections: (i) the ₡135.2 million previously reported as 1952 revenue is total budget *resources* (revenue plus the opening surplus); actual 1952 revenue was ₡129.7 million; (ii) the ₡140.3 million previously reported as 1953 revenue is total *credits used* (gastos plus compromisos); actual 1953 revenue was ₡137.6 million; (iii) the budget was in small deficit in both years, not in surplus; (iv) the 1953 ministry ranking, the expenditure-by-class tables, debt service and the tax shares were misread and are corrected below. ## Introduction

This note presents a synthesis of the economic and fiscal situation of El Salvador during the fiscal years 1952 and 1953, drawn from the *Informes Complementarios Constitucionales* (Memorias de Hacienda) published by the Ministry of Finance (*Ministerio de Hacienda*). These reports, submitted annually to the Legislative Assembly under Article 78 of the Constitution, are a rich primary source on the fiscal operations of the Salvadoran state in the mid-twentieth century. Where the 1952–53 commitments were executed in later years, the 1954 Memoria is also used.

The period corresponds to the early years of the Óscar Osorio administration (1950–1956), a modernizing military government that undertook significant reforms in public administration and fiscal management. The Memorias reveal a state building modern budgetary institutions while operating an economy highly dependent on coffee exports.

---

## 1. Budget Modernization

A recurring theme in both Memorias is the effort to modernize the budget process. Before 1952, budgets were built on *partidas específicas de egreso* — rigid line items allocating funds to narrowly defined expenditure categories. The Ministry noted that this system frequently impeded the normal operations of government agencies: some line items carried unspent balances while others, facing urgent needs, had no funds.

In 1952 the government introduced a transitional budget structure based on *apropiaciones o asignaciones globales* — broad appropriation classes — as a step toward the *Presupuesto por Actividades* (activity-based budgeting). The 1952 budget also eliminated the old distinction between fixed and variable expenditure.

By 1953 El Salvador had adopted the *presupuesto por actividades*. The 1953 Memoria emphasizes its advantages: the government could know, both at preparation and during execution, the cost and scale of each program and whether budgeted resources were sufficient. The new classification also distinguished operating, capital and transfer spending, which the Ministry explicitly linked to national-income analysis of the effect of public spending on the economy.

---

## 2. How the Memorias Measure the Budget

Reading these reports correctly requires four concepts that the Ministry keeps distinct:

| Concept | Meaning |
|:--|:--|
| *Rentas percibidas y devengadas* | Revenue of the year: mostly cash collections, plus customs duties on import/export policies liquidated but not yet collected, and (from 1952) direct taxes assessed but not yet paid |
| *Gastos* | Payment orders legalized and payments reported by paying offices during the year |
| *Compromisos pendientes de liquidación* | Credit reservations made against the year's budget but not yet paid (contracts, supply orders, advances for day labor) |
| *Créditos (asignaciones) utilizados* | Gastos + compromisos — the Ministry's measure of total budget execution |

The **budget result** is revenue minus credits used. The **Superávit final del presupuesto** adds the opening *Superávit Financiero* to revenue. The **Superávit Financiero** itself is a balance-sheet concept: current assets of the General Fund minus current liabilities, provisions and reserves.

The system was therefore a hybrid: revenue largely on a cash basis, expenditure charged when credit was reserved (an obligation basis). This matters for the analysis below, because commitments were paid over the following one to two years.

## 3. Revenue Structure and Performance

### 3.1 Revenue levels and growth

| | 1952 | 1953 |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Cash collections | ₡127,690,994.83 | — |
| Net customs policies pending collection | ₡1,998,963.70 | (not recorded) |
| **Rentas percibidas y devengadas (renta neta)** | **₡129,689,958.53** | **₡137,613,133.63** |
| Total rentas y otros ingresos (incl. capital and non-revenue receipts) | — | ₡140,541,243.07 |

On the Memoria's comparable basis (excluding pending customs policies), revenue rose by ₡12,791,557.79 in 1953, or **10%**. On the *renta neta* basis the increase was ₡7,923,175.10 (+6.1%); the 1953 figure is understated because customs policies pending at end-1953 were not recorded.

The 1953 report gives a five-year perspective on the same comparable basis:

| Fiscal Year | Revenue (₡ millions) | Increase over prior year |
|:-----------:|:--------------------:|:------------------------:|
| 1949        | 69.2                 | —                        |
| 1950        | 83.4                 | 21%                      |
| 1951        | 117.7                | 41%                      |
| 1952        | 127.7                | 8%                       |
| 1953        | 140.5                | 10%                      |

The Ministry attributed the doubling of revenue to the steady growth of economic activity, noting that with tax rates largely unchanged, revenue tended to follow national income.

### 3.2 Revenue composition

Revenue depended overwhelmingly on taxes, and within taxes on customs:

- In 1952, *Impuestos* yielded ₡106.4 million, **83.3%** of cash revenue. The *Renta de Aduanas* alone yielded ₡68.2 million, **53.4%** of cash revenue — by far the largest single source.
- In 1953, *Impuestos* represented **89.2%** of total income (the Memoria's figure, after excluding 1952 customs policies collected in 1953).
- *Impuestos Directos* yielded ₡11.0 million in 1952 (**8.6%** of cash revenue). They accounted for 39.4% of the year-on-year revenue increase in 1952, partly because direct taxes began to be recorded on an accrual basis that year.
- Other significant sources in 1952 were *Servicios Administrativos* (₡9.0 million, mostly consular fees), *Impuestos Varios* (₡9.0 million), *Especies sin Valor Comercial* (₡6.7 million) and *Explotación de Empresas Oficiales* (₡6.3 million).
- In 1953, the *Renta de Aduanas* (+₡6.1 million) and property-transfer taxes (+₡2.3 million) together explained about two-thirds of the net revenue increase.

### 3.3 Direct evidence of coffee sensitivity

Both Memorias attribute revenue shortfalls to coffee:

- **1952:** customs revenue came in below budget estimates because the 1951–52 coffee crop was smaller than forecast, and imports for domestic consumption fell correspondingly.
- **1953:** export duties were the main shortfall against estimates (₡3.0 million, 82% of all tax shortfalls), because the 1952–53 crop was smaller than forecast and exports of the 1953–54 crop began only a few days before year-end, instead of in November or early December as in previous years.

In both years the transmission ran through crop **volume** and timing, not only price: a smaller crop reduced export duties directly and import duties indirectly, through lower import capacity.

## 6. Budget Balance and the Financial Surplus

| ₡ millions | 1952 | 1953 |
|:--|--:|--:|
| Revenue (rentas percibidas y devengadas) | 129.69 | 137.61 |
| Credits used (gastos + compromisos) | 131.24 | 140.30 |
| **Budget result, commitment basis** | **−1.55** | **−2.68** |
| Opening Superávit Financiero (modified) | 5.46 | 6.72 |
| Superávit final del presupuesto | 3.92 | 4.04 |
| Payments on prior-year commitments | n.a. | 16.68 |
| **Budget result, cash basis (approx.)** | n.a. | **−3.12** |
| Superávit Financiero, 31 December | 4.06 | 5.47 |

The budget ran a **small deficit in both years**: credits used exceeded revenue by ₡1.55 million in 1952 and ₡2.68 million in 1953. The 1952 Memoria calls the gap a *déficit presupuestario*, smaller than that of 1951, and explains that the budget balanced only once the opening surplus is counted. A cash-basis cross-check for 1953 — current payments plus payments on earlier commitments, against revenue — gives a similar deficit of about ₡3.1 million.

The **Superávit Financiero** absorbed these deficits. It fell from ₡5.46 million to ₡4.06 million during 1952 and, after an upward revision to ₡6.72 million in January 1953 (mostly cancelled pre-1952 reservations), fell to ₡5.47 million by end-1953.

| External debt | 20,389,891.14 | 18,805,457.38 |

| Internal debt | 10,193,555.17 | 9,593,555.17 |
| **Total public debt** | **30,583,446.31** | **28,399,012.55** |

- Amortization in 1953: ₡3,584,433.81. Interest paid: ₡1,200,046.54 (1952) and ₡1,057,096.90 (1953).
- Total debt service charged to the budget: ₡4.66 million in 1952 (4.2% of payments) and ₡4.80 million in 1953 (3.9%).
- Internal debt at end-1953 consisted of the *Empréstito Interno de Aprovisionamiento* (₡4.0 million, amortized by ₡2.0 million in 1953), 5% Treasury Letters (₡4.0 million), Valle de La Esperanza bonds (₡1.4 million) and *quedanes fiscales* (₡0.19 million).
- The Government transferred ₡2.0 million to the Banco Central de Reserva for service of the 1922 loan, as required by the law reorganizing that debt.

The debt stock was modest (about 21–24% of annual revenue) and falling.

## 8. Economic Context

- **Coffee dependence:** Revenue relied heavily on customs, and both Memorias attribute shortfalls to the size and timing of the coffee crop. The fiscal position was highly sensitive to the coffee cycle through both export and import duties.
- **Modernization agenda:** Public works was the largest ministry, and dedicated funds were created for the Pan-American Highway, the Acajutla port and the Valle de La Esperanza project — a developmentalist orientation. Development ministries (education, health, public works, agriculture) absorbed about 41% of credits used, against 10–11% for defense.
- **Small deficits financed from accumulated surpluses:** The government did not borrow to cover its budget gaps; it drew on the Superávit Financiero, while repaying debt.
- **Institutional building:** The transition to activity-based budgeting, new government accounting, the requirement (from 1953) to reserve credit for all periodic expenditure, and detailed financial statements reflect a state building the infrastructure for modern fiscal management.

---

## Preliminary Observations

1. **Revenue buoyancy masked structural vulnerability.** Revenue doubled between 1949 and 1953, driven by the post-war and Korean War commodity boom. With more than half of revenue coming from customs, the fisc was exposed to the coffee crop and coffee prices.

2. **The budget was in recurrent small deficit, not surplus.** Spending commitments exceeded revenue in 1951, 1952 and 1953; the gap was covered by drawing down accumulated surpluses. This cushion was limited (₡4–7 million, against annual spending of ₡130–140 million).

3. **Investment was executed with long lags.** Public works generated most commitments, and a fifth of them were never executed. Investment was therefore the most elastic part of the budget in practice — a point to test against the coffee cycle.

4. **The development orientation was already in place in 1952–53.** Development ministries absorbed about four times the share of defense, a pattern usually associated with the 1954–57 boom years.

5. **Debt was not a constraint.** Service absorbed about 4% of payments and the stock was falling.
