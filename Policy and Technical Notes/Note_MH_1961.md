# El Salvador: Fiscal Year 1961

**From the Junta to the Directorio — Spending Through a Revenue Fall**

**Working Note and Narrative — Based on Primary Sources (November 2026)**

> **About this note.** This note covers the 1961 *Informe Complementario Constitucional* (signed 28 February 1962 by Humberto Bernal h., Ministro de Hacienda). It has two parts: the fiscal record by pillar, and a narrative reading of the year against the project's five hypotheses (H1–H5). Every fiscal figure was read on the page image and checked by arithmetic. The companion *Technical Note 1961* gives the full IMF-style diagnostic. Verified data are in "Spending Data/ministry_class_1961_1979.csv" and "Spending Data/pillars_1961_1979.csv".

Turcios describes the transition as two phases: the Junta pointed toward competitive elections without an official party (October 1960–January 1961), and the Directorio toward an "authoritarian reformism" shaped by anti-communism, official-party elections and a new constitution (1961–62) (Turcios, *Rebelión*, pp. 325–326).

The deficit was the largest of the series so far. It was covered by running down the State's accumulated surplus, selling securities (authorized up to ₡22.44M), borrowing between funds and delaying payments. The Memoria calls the last a "restriction of payments ... to meet others of greater importance or priority". There was almost no new debt.

## Part II — Narrative: Reading 1961 Against the Hypotheses

### The story in one paragraph

The Junta came to power on the back of an urban rebellion led by university students, professionals and organized workers. Its budget for 1961, voted two months later, raised education spending by almost a fifth. The Directorio that overthrew it in January executed that budget almost in full, even as revenue fell 7%. Instead of cutting, it spent the accumulated surplus, sold assets and delayed payments. It also reformed the income tax and took control of the Central Bank, building instruments for the next downturn. The squeeze fell on public investment, not on teachers or soldiers. **A plausible reading, to be tested in the following years: in 1961 the regime tried to win back the urban middle sectors it had lost in 1960, and paid for it with its balance sheet.**

### H1 — Was education (and the University) squeezed in the downturn?

**Not in 1961: the opposite.** Education's credits used rose 18.6% and its share reached 21.1%, the highest so far. Educación's subsidies, the line through which the University is most likely paid, nearly doubled (₡2.64M → ₡4.55M). The University is not separately identified in the Memoria, so the University-specific part of H1 is still open.

*Reading.* The Junta included civilian professionals from the opposition (Fabio Castillo Figueroa, René Fortín Magaña and Ricardo Falla Cáceres), and the coup's civilian organizers included the former University rector Romeo Fortín Magaña (Turcios, *Rebelión*, pp. 306–307). It owed its legitimacy to the student-led rebellion. A rise in education and University funding is consistent with that. That the Directorio kept the budget suggests it did not want an early fight with teachers and students. In the broader thesis, 1961 is a year of accommodation, not squeeze. The test of H1 moves to the years when the regime had no buffer left and had to choose.

### H2 — Were wages contained, especially teachers'?

**No.** The education wage bill rose 19.9% (₡23.19M → ₡27.80M). The total payroll rose 3.7% while revenue fell, reaching 56.9% of revenue. Defense wages rose 3.6% and police wages were about flat.

*Open question.* The Memoria cannot tell whether the education increase came from more posts or higher salaries per post. Turcios notes that by 1960 "in the teaching profession, posts and allowances were increasing" (*Rebelión*, p. 53). The 1961 budget law (D. 52, Diario Oficial 240, Tomo 189) lists posts and salaries and will settle this.

### H3 — Was public investment cut first?

**Yes.** Capital credits used fell about 15% (₡18.6M → ₡15.9M), public works fell 4.1%, and capital carry-forwards were halved. This is the same pattern as 1959. Two qualifications:

- **The flagship project continued outside the budget.** The IBRD-financed Litoral highway spent ₡12.7M in 1961, up from ₡7.5M in 1960.
- **Infrastructure was moving into autonomous institutions** (CEPA, CEL, and from October 1961 ANDA), financed by state guarantees that rose ₡13.3M. Budget investment therefore understates public investment; the off-budget part was protected.

*Reading.* Cutting the budget's own investment while protecting payroll and keeping the export-linked infrastructure (port, highway, electricity) off budget is a consistent pattern from 1959 onward. Its cost fell on smaller works, such as rural roads, water and school buildings financed from the ordinary budget, which are the projects most visible to the rural population.

### H4 — Monetary tightening?

**Not testable from the Memoria.** What 1961 shows is the institutional change: the Central Bank became a state entity in April with powers to set reserve requirements and credit ceilings (D. 116), and its Organic Law (D. 496) in December made it responsible for monetary, credit and exchange policy. Interest rates, credit and international reserves for 1960–62 must come from the BCR's *Revista mensual*.

*Reading.* Before 1961 the fixed exchange rate and a privately owned central bank left the government with only the budget to absorb coffee shocks. From 1961 it had a monetary instrument. Whether it was used to tighten (protecting reserves) or to finance the State is the key question for 1962 onward.

### H5 — Were IRA food subsidies cut?

**Indirect evidence only.** The State's guarantee of the IRA's Central Bank credit fell from ₡5.99M to ₡4.34M, which suggests a smaller grain-stabilization operation. The budget transfer to the IRA is not separately identified (it is probably inside the Ramo de Economía's ₡3.07M of subsidies). IRA reports are needed.

### Security

The Defense-controlled security apparatus held its share (12.4% of credits used against 12.6% in 1960). Its money went to wages (76% of Defense payments, 86% of Seguridad Pública); equipment stayed small (₡0.33M in Defense). The presidency, now the Directorio, grew 26%. The territorial-control units of Interior (Gobernaciones Departamentales and Migración, ₡0.59M) were flat. Nothing in the 1961 budget suggests a build-up of the security apparatus. Control in 1961 worked through politics (the outlawing of the Communist Party, the reorganization of the official party) rather than through the budget.

### What 1961 sets up

- **No buffer for the next shock.** Coffee prices fell another 7% in 1962. With the Superávit Financiero at ₡0.5M, the 1962 government would have to cut spending, raise taxes or borrow. The September 1961 income-tax reform and the new Central Bank were the tools it had prepared.
- **A larger, more rigid budget.** Payroll was 57% of revenue, and education was a fifth of the budget. Teachers and the University now had more at stake in the budget, which is the precondition of the project's thesis that later adjustment would turn them against the regime.
- **A shift of investment off budget**, into autonomous institutions backed by state guarantees.
