# El Salvador: Fiscal Year 1962

**Authoritarian Reformism's First Budget — Taxes, Central Bank Credit and the Alliance for Progress**

**Working Note and Narrative — Based on Primary Sources (November 2026)**

> **About this note.** This note covers the 1962 *Informe Complementario Constitucional* (signed 28 February 1963 by Alvaro Marino, Ministro de Hacienda). Part I gives the fiscal record by pillar; Part II reads the year against the project's hypotheses and against the expectation set out after the 1961 note: that the 1962 budget, the Directorio's own, would show "a shift away from the University and toward security and investment". Political periods follow Roberto Turcios's terms: the Junta (October 1960–January 1961) as the opening toward **democratic transition**, and the Directorio Cívico Militar and its successors as **authoritarian reformism** (*reformismo autoritario*; Turcios, *Rebelión*, pp. 325–326). The full diagnostic is in the companion *Technical Note 1962*.

## Part II — Narrative: Did 1962 Bring a New Direction?

### The story in one paragraph

Authoritarian reformism inherited an empty treasury and a budget enlarged by the Junta. It did not reverse the Junta's education expansion: education held its record share and teachers' pay rose again. It adjusted instead on three fronts that the earlier governments had not used together. It taxed higher incomes (the income tax rose 40%). It cut the ordinary budget's own investment by a third. And it financed the remaining gap with credit from the Central Bank it had just nationalized. At the same time it opened a new channel of resources outside the ordinary budget: Alliance-for-Progress grants, US farm-surplus credit, Eximbank and DLF loans for the airport, and IDB loans guaranteed by the State. **The regime's response to fiscal stress was to modernize its instruments rather than to squeeze the urban middle sectors, at least in 1962.**

### Testing the expectation: "away from the University, toward security and investment"

| Expected | What 1962 shows | Verdict |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Away from the University | Educación's subsidies, the line that most likely carries the University transfer, fell 22% (₡4.55M → ₡3.53M), while teachers' salaries rose 13.6% | **Partly supported** — a cut in the likely University line, but not in education as a whole |
| Toward security | Security credits −0.7%; share 12.4% → 12.5%; payroll flat; equipment halved; the police absorbed into the Ramo de Defensa | **Not supported in money; supported institutionally** |
| Toward investment | Ordinary-budget capital −35%, public works −17%; but aid- and loan-financed extraordinary budgets started (airport ₡9.7M committed, school buildings, PL-480) | **Not in the ordinary budget; yes off budget, with foreign money** |

Two findings were not anticipated:

- **The presidency grew by a third.** Its payments rose to ₡5.1M, including ₡1.07M of *gratificaciones, subsidios y recompensas*, a class that was almost empty in 1961. The Memoria does not explain these payments. In a year of transition to an official-party presidency, discretionary money at the presidency is worth tracing in the budget law.
- **Security was reorganized, not enlarged.** The separate Ramo de Seguridad Pública disappeared and the police became "Cuerpos de Seguridad Pública", a unit of the Ministry of Defense. This confirms, in the budget itself, the author's point that the police were Defense bodies. The control apparatus was consolidated under the military command at constant cost.

### The hypotheses

**H1 — Education and the University squeezed.** *Mixed.* Education overall was protected (+2.5%, record 21.9% share). The probable University line was cut 22%. If the budget law confirms that the cut fell on the University, 1962 is the first year in which the regime separated the two constituencies: teachers kept (and gained), the University lost. That is consistent with the University's role in the 1960 rebellion and with the Junta's composition.

**H2 — Wage containment.** *Not for teachers; yes for casual labor.* Permanent salaries rose 8.4% overall and 13.6% in primary education; casual and temporary labor (*Otros Servicios Personales*) fell 12%. Defense and police payroll was flat. The posts-versus-salary question remains open until the 1962 budget law is read.

**H3 — Investment cut first.** *Strongly supported.* Capital credits fell 35% after a 15% cut in 1961 — down about 45% in two years (₡18.6M → ₡10.3M) — and public works fell 17%. The regime protected payroll and cut its own investment, while bringing in foreign-financed investment through extraordinary budgets.

**H4 — Monetary tightening.** *The opposite, for the government.* The Central Bank lent the Treasury ₡15.5M through promissory notes. The instrument created by the 1961 reform was used in its first full year to finance the deficit, not to tighten. Whether the BCR tightened credit to the private sector at the same time needs its *Revista mensual*.

**H5 — IRA food subsidies cut.** *Not supported.* The IRA's state-guaranteed credit rose 75% (₡4.34M → ₡7.61M), suggesting larger grain-stabilization operations. The Ramo de Economía's subsidies fell from ₡3.07M to ₡1.30M, but the recipients are not identified in the Memoria.

### What 1962 sets up

- **A new fiscal settlement.** Revenue less dependent on trade taxes (48% from 56% in 1960), thanks to the income tax.
- **Two budgets.** An ordinary budget mainly made of payroll (55% of payments), and a growing extraordinary budget financed by foreign lenders and donors, governed by the Alliance for Progress. The pillar analysis must now track both.
- **Central Bank financing as a tool.** The next coffee downturn will test whether it becomes routine.
