# El Salvador: Fiscal Year 1968 — The Third Lean Year: Investment Halved, the Army Funded Off-Budget, the Teachers on Strike

**Working Note — Evidence from the Informe Complementario Constitucional (November 2026)**

> **About this note.** This is the working note on the 1968 Memoria, in the format of the 1952–1967 series. It ends with a section on the hypotheses (H1–H5) and the four tracks proposed in the *Political Economy Context* note: the tax bargain, the Honduras safety valve, the protection bargain, and reforms diluted. Every figure was read on the page image and checked by arithmetic. Political periods follow Turcios (authoritarian reformism, now under Sánchez Hernández). The structural reading draws on Pleitez, Dada Hirezi and the conflict literature. The IMF-style diagnostic is in the companion *Technical Note 1968*.

**Political economy reading of 1968.** Four strands from the *Political Economy Context* note meet in this year.

- **The political opening was at its peak.** In Almeida's terms, institutional access peaked: the PDC won 19 of 52 seats and kept San Salvador. The same year brought the first national teachers' strike, in a year when education spending *rose* (section 2.3). The conflict was over pay, pensions and how the reform was designed (Lindo-Fuentes and Ching), not over the total budget.
- **Integration was under strain.** The Common Market's protocol imposed a stabilization tax on imports. The Memoria shows customs revenue falling to a third of revenue, its lowest share in the series. The model of import substitution and the CACM was losing fiscal yield (Pleitez; Dada Hirezi).
- **The army was funded off-budget while public works were cut.** The Treasury found ₡10M in domestic bonds for military equipment, outside the ordinary Defence budget, in a year when Public Works was cut 26% and the capital budget halved. This is the kind of priority Stanley's "protection racket" thesis predicts. It also came more than a year before the Honduras war (see *Note_Military_Financing_1967_1969*).
- **Coffee and the tax bargain.** The coffee price was flat, yet the coffee export duty fell 21.5%. Since 1950 coffee has paid mainly at the border (D. 836, which exempted income from coffee land from income tax; whether that exemption survived the 1963 income tax law is still to be checked). When the border tax weakens, the coffee sector's contribution falls with it. In 1968 the gap was filled by a property-type tax (the *Impuesto de Vialidad*, nearly tripled) and by wage withholding.

Currency: US$1.00 = ₡2.50.

## Introduction: what the 1968 volume contains

The 1968 Memoria (114 scanned pages) contains:

- the narrative (printed pp. 17–51; page 30 is printed upside down in the scan);
- Estados II-4 and II-5 (1968 revenue, with 1967 and 1966);
- **only the first page of Estado II-6** (the liquidation of the 1968 budget by unit);
- Estado IV-2 (the 1967 budget at final execution, already used in the 1967 note);
- Estados VII-1 and VII-2 (debt).

**The 1968 table of spending by ministry and unit is not in the scans.** For spending by ministry this note uses **Estado II-8 of the 1969 Memoria**. That table compares the credits used (gastos plus commitments) by ramo for 1967, 1968 and 1969. All three columns sum exactly to their totals.

**The basis therefore changes from the 1967 note.** That note compared ministries at final execution. Here both years are on the **credits-used basis** from a single table, so 1967 and 1968 are comparable. Spending at the level of individual units (the Fuerza Armada and police split, the University, primary education, the Presidency's *Administración Particular*, the IRA) is not available for 1968, except where the narrative gives it.

## 1. Revenue Structure and Performance

### 1.1 Revenue

**Current revenue was ₡230,982,446.37, +1.8% over 1967's ₡226.91M.**

- **A note on bases.** The Memoria reports +1.2%, but it compares ₡230.98M with ₡228.15M. The 1967 figure includes the AID malaria loan (₡1.25M); the 1968 figure excludes the ₡2.0M drawn on the same loan. On a like-for-like basis the growth is 1.8%.
- **Against the estimate.** Revenue was ₡9.05M (3.8%) below the estimate of ₡240.03M.
- **Capital revenue** was only ₡1.25M, against ₡5.99M estimated: AID drawings of ₡1.18M and asset sales of ₡0.07M.
- **Total income** was ₡234.23M.

**The revenue base shifted from the border to direct taxes.**

- **Customs fell to 33.5% of current revenue,** against 39.6% in 1967 and 45.4% in 1964 (Memoria, p. 25). The Memoria calls the drop "marked".
- **Two direct taxes made up the loss:**
  - the income tax (+₡6.56M);
  - the *Impuesto de Vialidad Serie "A"*, classified as a tax on capital, which nearly tripled (+₡9.13M, +142% on the Memoria's figures).
- **The coffee duty fell 21.5% while the price was flat.** The Memoria explains that exports subject to duty were irregular in October–December, the first quarter of the 1968–69 coffee year: the quarter's export duties came to only ₡2.21M. The progressive *ad valorem* scale of 1950 also means the duty falls faster than the price at the bottom of the cycle. **[DATA GAP: coffee export volumes and unit values by quarter, BCR.]**
- **Import duties fell 10.9%.** The largest falls were on the lines exempted under the Ley de Fomento Industrial:
  - materials-classified manufactures −17.5%;
  - machinery and transport equipment −18.1%.

  They include ₡1.38M of the new stabilization tax, from 9 November.
- **Arrears in tax payments.** More than 1,700 taxpayers obtained instalment plans for about ₡9.50M of income tax and ₡4.42M of Vialidad (p. 50). Taxes receivable rose from ₡16.83M to ₡19.24M.

**The rise in personal income tax was largely an accounting change.** From 1968, the tax withheld from permanent salaries was credited directly to the General Fund. The 1969 Memoria (p. 27) states that this credited ₡6.59M to the 1968 income tax, "part of which had remained immobilized as third-party funds". Excluding those ₡6.59M, personal income tax would have been about ₡21.7M, flat on 1967.

**Company income tax was flat, still 21% below 1964 (₡14.21M).** Individuals paid 63% of the income tax. That is the highest share in the series, and much of it is salary withholding, including from public employees.

**The duties that fell most are those on the industrial inputs and capital goods covered by the Fomento Industrial exemptions.** Duties on food, which are not exempted, kept rising.

Following the *Political Economy Context* note, the tax-expenditure account should now carry three items:

1. **The Fomento Industrial exemptions** (duties and income tax).
2. **Regional free trade within the CACM.**
3. **The coffee income-tax exemption of 1950 (D. 836),** if it remained in force after the 1963 income tax law.

None is quantified in the Memorias. The three estimation routes proposed in the 1967 note still apply: the effective tariff, the composition of duties, and firm-level grants in the Diario Oficial.

## 8. Preliminary Observations

1. **Third lean year.** The coffee price was flat at its low, but the coffee duty fell 21.5%. Revenue grew 1.8% only because of a near-tripling of the Vialidad tax and the transfer of wage withholding into the General Fund.
2. **Customs fell to a third of revenue,** under the combined weight of the coffee cycle, regional free trade and the Fomento Industrial exemptions. The new CACM stabilization tax is a first sign of the model's fiscal limits.
3. **The capital budget was halved** (−52% gastos). Public Works fell 26% and Economía 46%. Education and Health grew 9–11%, carried by salaries, hospitals and the University.
4. **Payroll reached 59% of spending.** The Memoria itself attributes this to the running costs of earlier investment (more teachers and doctors).
5. **The army was equipped off-budget** with ₡10M of domestic bonds. With that budget, security spending rose 36% while development rose 1.5%.
6. **The Treasury financed itself through arrears to the ISSS (₡10.2M) and to international organizations**, and through more Treasury notes (₡10M) and third-party funds.
7. **Teachers struck in a year of rising education spending.** The Government's response included a medical-services law financed by the teachers' own 2% contribution.

## 9. Reading 1968 Against the Hypotheses

| Hypothesis | 1968 evidence | Verdict |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **H1** Education and the University squeezed | Education +9.2% (credits used); University +17% (₡11.25M); education's share 26.7%, a new high | **Not supported.** Education was protected and expanded with the reform. Conflict came over pay, pensions and how the reform was designed, not the budget total |
| **H2** Wage containment, especially teachers | Payroll +4.0% (permanent salaries +4.9%); 59% of gastos; ₡10.2M of employer contributions to the ISSS unpaid; teachers' medical services financed by a 2% deduction from their pay | **Partly supported.** Wage growth was moderate. Part of the labour cost was shifted to employees and to arrears. Teachers' posts and pay are still to be measured (Diario Oficial) |
| **H3** Public investment cut first | Capital budget −52% (gastos), −42% (credits used); construction −55%; capital transfers −64%; Public Works −26% | **Strongly supported, for the third year running** |
| **H4** Monetary policy | Treasury notes ₡6M → ₡10M; ₡10M bonds placed through the BCR as fiscal agent; third-party funds lent to the Treasury | **Accommodation continues.** Domestic credit financed the Treasury and the army. Holders of the notes and bonds are to be identified (BCR) |
| **H5** IRA food subsidies cut | Not observable: no unit data for 1968. IRA borrowed ₡0.55M from the Fondo Especial; its 1969 subvención was ₡0.50M against ₡0.70M in 1967 | **Probably continued to fall** (to be confirmed) |
| *Track A: tax bargain* | Coffee duty −21.5% at a flat price; company income tax flat; growth came from Vialidad and wage withholding | Burden shifted toward property and salaried taxpayers; industrial exemptions are visible in the duty composition |
| *Track B: safety valve* | No fiscal trace yet. Pressure on Salvadoran settlers in Honduras was building toward the April 1969 evictions | To follow in 1969 |
| *Track C: protection bargain* | ₡10M off-budget for the army while public works were cut 26%; security +36% including the extraordinary budget | **The first clear case in the series** |
| *Track D: reform diluted* | — | Nothing new in 1968 |

**The story in one paragraph.** In 1968 the Sánchez Hernández government met a third lean year by halving investment. It closed what remained of the gap with arrears to the social security institute and short-term Treasury notes. It shifted revenue toward direct taxes on property and wages, as customs and coffee yields fell. It kept expanding education and health, because the buildings of the loan-financed boom now needed staff. The political opening was at its peak, with the PDC at 19 seats and teachers striking for the first time nationally. At the same time the State found ₡10M outside the ordinary budget to equip the army. **The pattern shifts from "protect people, cut buildings and food" (1966–67) to "protect people, cut buildings, and arm off-budget".** The question for 1969 is how the war with Honduras, and the closing of the migration outlet, changed that balance.
