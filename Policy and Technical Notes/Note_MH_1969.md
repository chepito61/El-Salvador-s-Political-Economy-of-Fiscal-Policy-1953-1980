# El Salvador: Fiscal Year 1969 — The War Year: Arms Paid with Patriotic Bonds and Central Bank Notes

**Working Note — Evidence from the Informe Complementario Constitucional (November 2026)**

**Political economy reading of 1969.** Four strands from the *Political Economy Context* note meet in this year.

- **The safety valve closed (track B).** Dada Hirezi and Durham see emigration to Honduras as a structural outlet for El Salvador's landless. The 1918 treaty provided for it, and the Common Market's free movement of people extended it. The evictions and the war closed that outlet. Between 60,000 and 130,000 people came back, into a country where landless rural families were already about a third of the total (Pleitez, Table 2.17).
- **The return left no trace in the budget.** The 1969 Memoria records no spending on the returnees: there are no lines for resettlement, emergency housing or land. **The war's fiscal cost was military, and the cost of the returnees was left to the families and the labour market.** Pleitez lists the repatriation from Honduras among the causes of the rise in labour under-utilization in the 1970s.
- **The protection bargain (track C).** The State raised ₡31.4M in two months for the emergency, through a one-time tax and patriotic bonds, and borrowed ₡20M from the Central Bank. Of the emergency spending paid in 1969, **92% was "machinery and equipment"**, that is, arms. The 1968 off-budget purchase (₡10M) had anticipated this.
- **The regional model broke (Pleitez; Dada Hirezi).** The CACM, the second engine of the import-substitution model, lost its Honduran market. The Memoria again blames the erosion of import duties on the incentives of the Ley de Fomento (p. 28).

**A political note.** The war was popular at home. The regime's standing rose, and the PCN won the March 1970 legislative elections with 34 seats (DADM). The fiscal effort was presented as "patriotic": the bonds were named for national dignity. The opposition's 1964–68 gains were halted. To be followed in 1970.

Currency: US$1.00 = ₡2.50.

The 1969 Memoria (72 scanned pages, mostly turned sideways in the scan) contains:

- the narrative (printed pp. 17–60);
- Estados II-4 and II-5 (revenue 1969, 1968 and 1967);
- **Estado II-7** (1969 spending by unit and class, gastos basis, total ₡247,222,412.53);

- the debt statements.

**Two bases are used here:**

- **Credits used (Estado II-8)** for ramos, comparable with the 1967 and 1968 columns.
- **Gastos (Estado II-7)** for units inside ramos and for economic classes.

**A correction to the 1967 note.** The 1969 Memoria's revenue history (p. 30) shows that 1967 current revenue (₡226.905M) **"includes ₡4.50M of income tax withholdings transferred to the [General] Fund"**. In 1968 a further ₡6.59M of withholdings was credited, part of it from earlier years. These one-off transfers flatter revenue growth in 1967–68. Underlying current revenue was about ₡222.4M in 1967, little more than 1966.

## 1. Revenue Structure and Performance

### 1.1 Revenue

**Ordinary current revenue was ₡242.65M, +5.1%,** the best growth since 1965 (+₡11.67M).

- **Emergency revenue of ₡31.42M** came on top:
  - **emergency tax:** ₡9.30M, a tax;

  - sale of the old Penitenciaría Central site to the Central Bank: ₡2.04M;

**Customs revenue.**

- **The coffee duty rose 12.1%** on a price up only 1.1%. That reverses the 1968 shortfall, when the October–December 1968 shipments were delayed.
- **Customs recovered to ₡84.60M (34.9% of revenue),** but only because of the stabilization tax. Without it customs would have been ₡73.91M (31.8%). The Memoria counts a decline of 17.6% since 1965 and attributes it "especially [to] the granting of fiscal incentives to firms that have received benefits under the Ley de Fomento" (p. 28).
- **The stabilization tax fell mostly on manufactures** (₡3.49M), machinery and transport equipment (₡3.28M) and other manufactured goods (₡1.88M). In practice it partly offset the Fomento exemptions on imports from outside Central America.

**A trace of the 1950 coffee regime.** The Interior budget still pays the municipalities a subsidy "to compensate for the suppression of taxes on the cultivation of coffee and for other municipal activities": ₡352,397.08 in 1969 (Estado II-7, Ramo del Interior). **This is the largest deficit relative to revenue in the 1960–69 series,** slightly above 1961 (−15.4%).

- **Excluding the war, the deficit would have been modest (−3.4%).**
- **The Memoria's own figure of −₡9.68M treats the patriotic bonds as revenue.** Its claim that without the emergency the result "would have been practically in balance" (p. 45) holds only on that treatment.
- **The operating saving has been falling.** The Memoria explains that current revenue rose 8.1% from 1965 to 1969 while current spending (excluding the emergency) rose 19.6%. "The operating saving destined for the Capital Budget has been gradually shrinking, making the financing of the Central Government's investments more difficult" (p. 39).

**How the war deficit was covered.** The deficit was met by:

- the *Bonos de la Dignidad Nacional* (₡22.12M);
- ₡10M more of Central Bank notes;
- the 7% public-investment bonds (₡3.50M);
- running the Superávit Financiero down by ₡10.0M, to −₡9.91M.

The rest was carried into 1970 as unpaid commitments (₡44.67M, of which ₡27.20M for the emergency).

## 8. Preliminary Observations

1. **The coffee cycle bottomed out.** The price was flat at its low, yet ordinary revenue grew 5.1%, helped by the CACM stabilization tax (₡10.7M) and higher consumption taxes.
2. **The war cost ₡41.0M in credits used (14% of the budget).** It was financed by a one-time emergency tax (₡9.3M), patriotic bonds (₡22.1M) and Central Bank notes (₡10M more). The recorded deficit (−₡39.9M) is the largest in the 1960s.
3. **The money paid out for the emergency went 90% on machinery and equipment.** The ordinary army line did not grow, and the police line has been flat since 1966.
4. **Development spending was not cut to pay for the war.** Education +6.6%, health +6.8%, public works +35.6%. The adjustment margin of 1966–68 (investment) was reopened with domestic bonds.
5. **The IRA subsidy kept falling** (−29% since 1967). The two presidential discretionary lines reached ₡2.24M.
6. **No budget line addressed the 60,000–130,000 returnees from Honduras.**
7. **Domestic debt more than doubled, mostly for military purposes.** The Treasury became openly dependent on the Central Bank.

## 9. Reading 1969 Against the Hypotheses

| Hypothesis | 1969 evidence | Verdict |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **H1** Education and the University squeezed | Education +6.6%; University +14% (₡12.85M incl. capital); share falls to 22.9% only because the war inflated the total | **Not supported:** protected in money, diluted in share |
| **H2** Wage containment, especially teachers | Payroll +3.5%, the third year of growth near 4%; primary education unit +8.5% over two years (mixed bases); teachers paid ₡1.73M into their own medical fund | **Supported in a mild form:** slow wage growth and costs shifted to employees, with no cuts in pay |
| **H3** Public investment cut first | Capital budget +38% (credits used) after three years of cuts, financed partly by domestic bonds | **Not in 1969:** investment was restored as revenue recovered |
| **H4** Monetary policy | Treasury notes held by the Central Bank doubled to ₡20M; Superávit Financiero negative (−₡9.9M) | **Monetary financing of the war:** accommodation, not tightening |
| **H5** IRA food subsidies cut | IRA ₡0.50M (−29% since 1967), less than the tourism institute | **Supported:** a steady decline since 1966 |
| *Track A: tax bargain* | War financed by a one-time tax on incomes and liquid capital and by voluntary bonds. Company income tax flat. The 1950 coffee compensation to municipalities still paid | **Burden on salaried and propertied households and on bondholders, not on companies** |
| *Track B: safety valve* | 60,000–130,000 returnees; no fiscal response | **The valve closed; the State did not absorb the shock** |
| *Track C: protection bargain* | ₡41M emergency (90% equipment) on top of the ₡10M of 1968; financed by patriotic bonds and the Central Bank | **Strongly visible** |
| *Track D: reform diluted* | — | Nothing new in 1969 |

**The story in one paragraph.** At the bottom of the coffee cycle, El Salvador went to war with Honduras. The State paid for the war outside its ordinary budget, with a one-time tax, patriotic bonds and Central Bank notes. Most of the cash went on arms. It did not cut schools, hospitals or roads to do it, and it restored investment. **The war was fought on credit, and the social shock of the war was not budgeted at all.** Tens of thousands of landless returnees came back to a country with no budget line for them. Meanwhile the food subsidy kept shrinking and wages grew slowly. **The pattern for 1969 is "protect the budget, arm on credit, leave the returnees to the market".** For 1970, the questions are how the new debt and the Central Bank notes are serviced, and whether the returnees and the loss of the Honduran market show up in agrarian policy (the 1970 Congreso de Reforma Agraria) and in revenue.
