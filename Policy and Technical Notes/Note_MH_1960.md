## Political Context

The fiscal year is the last of the PRUD regime founded in 1948–50 (Osorio 1950–56, Lemus 1956–60). The Legislative Assembly voted the 1960 budget on 23 December 1959 (D.L. 2983). Lemus executed it for ten months. A military–civilian Junta de Gobierno overthrew him on 26 October 1960 and executed the last two months. The Junta was itself overthrown on 25 January 1961 by the Directorio Cívico Militar, under which Minister of Finance Ernesto A. Núñez signed the Informe on 28 February 1961.

Turcios reads 1960 as the year that "closed the chapter of modernizing authoritarianism": a regime that had built ports, dams, roads and institutions while repressing almost everyone suspected of organizing discontent, and that in the second half of the decade tried to open elections and push social reform. That attempt lasted "until August" (*Rebelión*, pp. 325–326).

**Political economy reading.** Three threads tie 1960 to the research question.

1. **The bust came first, the crisis second.** Coffee export duties fell from ₡54.6M in 1957 to ₡35.5M in 1958 and ₡24.8M in 1959. Turcios's *Autoritarismo* (pp. 146–147) records the regime's own arithmetic: each dollar off the world price cost the State ₡1,187,500, in a budget of ₡181M, and the fall forced the partial reform of coffee policy, the 1957 International Coffee Agreement, and a hurried turn to Central American integration. The fiscal adjustment of 1958–59 (two deficits, two years of falling payments, investment cut) preceded the political crisis of 1960. In 1960 itself revenue recovered. The crisis broke in a year of fiscal recovery, after a bust, which is the sequence predicted by Acemoglu and Robinson (2001).
2. **Reform offered, reform withdrawn.** Lemus's response to the bust and to the Cuban revolution was a Christian-democratic reformism of his own: a rural minimum wage, a minimum food ration for farm workers, the Metalío smallholdings, and an alliance with the Church against communism. The coffee growers answered with their own fiscal demand, to convert the coffee duty into an income tax. None of the laws was passed before the coup; Lemus said the minimum-wage law would have been voted the day after he fell (*Rebelión*, p. 310). In the terms of Acemoglu and Robinson (2000), the concession was announced but never committed.
3. **Development and security in the same months.** The year's budget gave education its largest share of the decade (18.6% of credits used) while the security apparatus held flat at 12.6%. In August–September the regime used that apparatus against the University its budget was expanding: the assault of 2 September was carried out by the forces of the Ministry of Defense against the institution whose students and staff the development pillar was producing. Turcios's verdict is that "the rebellion of 1960 defeated the regime, but a coup conspiracy overthrew it" (p. 325). The Junta that followed included a University-linked physician (Fabio Castillo) and the son of a former rector; its successor, the Directorio, rebuilt the regime as "authoritarian reformism".

Currency: US$1.00 = ₡2.50 (colones), fixed exchange rate.

## Introduction

The 1960 volume is complete: narrative pp. 3–41, and Estados 1–28, including Estado 4 (credits used by unit, with the gastos/compromisos split), Estado 5 (spending by unit and class), Estado 12 (payments on prior-year budgets), Estado 16 (Superávit Financiero), and Estados 19–21 (debt, debt service and guarantees). ## 1. Revenue Structure and Performance

### 1.1 The partial recovery

*Rentas percibidas y devengadas* were **₡170,546,506.01**; with *otros ingresos* (₡437,860.44), total income was ₡170,984,366.45. Rentas rose ₡13,876,350.49 (**+8.9%**) over 1959 (₡156,670,155.52), ending two years of decline. Total income exceeded the budget estimate by ₡6,995,760.45 (4.3%). Rentas remained 10.0% below the 1957 peak (₡189.5M).

The recovery came through **imports**. Import duties rose ₡11.5M (+19.7%) to ₡69.8M and explained 63.2% of all revenue increases. Coffee export duties rose only 6.0%, to ₡26.28M (₡24.77M in 1959), less than half their 1957 level.

### 1.3 The income tax

Renta y Vialidad fell for the first time since 1955, by ₡2,655,618.79 (−13.5%). The Memoria explains about half of it: ₡1,248,658.19 of assessments for 1952–55 had been booked in 1959 as a one-off. Net of it, the decline was ₡1,406,960.60 (about −7.6%), consistent with a lag of income-tax collections behind the 1958–59 downturn, a reading the Memoria does not make.

The year's tax debate ran the other way. When the government proposed the rural minimum wage, the Ahuachapán coffee and grain growers asked for "tax equality": a merchant earning ₡6,000 was exempt from income tax, while a grower with the same net income paid about ₡3,400 through the coffee duty (₡16 a quintal). They asked to replace the duty with an income tax (*Rebelión*, p. 173). The demand is the coffee elite's side of the tax bargain that the 1961 income-tax reform would reopen.

## 5. Monetary Conditions

The 1960 Memoria says nothing about monetary policy, and the Central Bank (BCR) was still a private-shareholder institution: it became a state entity only in April 1961 (D. 116). Its links with the Treasury in 1960 were three:

1. **Fiscal agent.** The BCR held ₡4,229,759.82 for the service of the 1922 loan; net of it, public debt was ₡31,637,069.59. The Treasury paid it "cuotas compensatorias" as fiscal agent.
2. **Treasury investment in the BCR.** Part of the ₡17.4M of short-term investments was BCR participation certificates.
3. **No Treasury borrowing from the BCR.** The debt statement shows no Central Bank credit to the Government, in contrast with 1969 onward.

**Reserves and the stand-by.** Turcios reports, from the BCR, that international reserves fell from ₡119.4M in 1954 to ₡114.7M in 1958 and ₡89.9M in 1959, recovered to ₡110.2M in July 1960, and then fell to ₡95.6M in August and ₡82M in September, ending the year at the lowest level of the decade. An IMF stand-by of US$11.25M (₡28.1M) was approved to support reserves; BCR president Carlos Canessa said similar operations had been made in the previous four years (*Rebelión*, pp. 115–116). The fall coincides with the political crisis of August–October and suggests capital flight under a fixed exchange rate. The monthly BCR balance sheet is needed to confirm the figures and the year-end level.

**National accounts.** The BCR published El Salvador's first full national accounts in 1960, for 1958: gross territorial product ₡1,287.6M, gross national product ₡1,267.2M; agriculture 38%, commerce 21%, industry 12%, public administration 11%, services 18% (*Rebelión*, p. 116). On that basis 1958 revenue (₡167.7M) was about 13% of GNP.

Debt fell ₡4.0M (−10.2%) to 20.9% of revenue, for the third year running. Debt service paid was ₡7.60M (principal ₡5.84M, interest ₡1.77M), 4.5% of revenue. The State was reducing its own debt while lending its credit to the autonomous sector.

## 7. Extraordinary Budgets and Loan-Financed Investment

The **Carretera del Litoral** was carried in an extraordinary budget financed by the IBRD and the General Fund. From 1955 to 1960 the General Fund contributed ₡38.8M and the IBRD ₡28.58M; total spending reached ₡68.14M, of which ₡7.46M in 1960. A new IBRD loan for access roads began to disburse (₡1.37M). The two large works of the period outside the budget, the Lempa hydroelectric dam (CEL) and the Acajutla port (CEPA, whose last of 79 caissons was placed in September 1960), were financed through the autonomous agencies and appear only as guarantees and as Treasury holdings of their bonds.

Other investments in 1960: IRA ₡856,000, INSAFOP ₡500,000, and the first subscription to the Inter-American Development Bank (₡66,000 and more).

## 9. Preliminary Observations

1. **Revenue recovered, but through imports.** Rentas rose 8.9%; import duties explained 63% of the gain, coffee duties stayed below half their 1957 level, and trade taxes rose to 56% of revenue.
2. **The income tax fell, mostly for accounting reasons**, while the coffee growers asked to replace the export duty with an income tax.
3. **Development peaked, security was flat.** Development took 45.7% of credits used (education 18.6%), security 12.6%. The repression of August–October left no trace in the year's budget.
4. **The deficit nearly closed and the surplus rose** to ₡20.3M. The fiscal accounts do not show a government that had run out of money at the time of the coup.
5. **But liquidity was thin.** Cash fell to 15.9% of current assets; the surplus was held in receivables and in bonds of the autonomous agencies.
6. **Reserves fell with the political crisis.** The drop from July to September 1960 and the IMF stand-by are the first monetary trace of a political shock in the series.
7. **Contingent liabilities grew** to ₡182.5M as direct debt fell: the infrastructure state was built on guarantees.
8. **1960 closes the first chapter.** In the Osorio–Lemus decade rentas doubled, the development pillar rose from 38.7% to 45.7% of spending, security fell, and the institutional budget system survived two coups.

## 10. Reading 1960 Against the Hypotheses

| Hypothesis | 1960 evidence | Verdict |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **H1** Education and the University squeezed in downturns | 1960 was a recovery year; education +11.2%, record share 18.6%; no squeeze in the 1958–59 bust either | **Not applicable / rejected for 1958–60** |
| **H2** Wage containment, especially teachers | Total payroll flat (₡87.0M → ₡86.8M); teachers' posts and pay not yet separated | **Open** — Ley de Salarios needed |
| **H3** Public investment cut first | Cut in 1958–59; in 1960 public works +8.2% and capital commitments up | **Supported for the bust; recovery in 1960** |
| **H4** Monetary tightening | No BCR credit to the Treasury; reserves fall July–September; IMF stand-by | **Open** — BCR monthly data needed |
| **H5** IRA food subsidies cut | IRA guarantee ₡5.46M → ₡5.99M; ₡856,000 invested | **Rejected for 1960** |
| **PE1** Concessions temporary, not committed | Minimum wage and food-ration bills sent in August; not passed before the coup | **Supported** |
| **PE2** Busts raise the threat | Crisis of 1960 follows the 1958–59 coffee bust, in a recovery year | **Supported (timing)** |
| **PE3** Repression cheap until it was not | Assault and state of siege with a flat security budget | **Supported** |
| **PE4** Polarization keeps fiscal capacity narrow | Trade taxes 56% of revenue; growers seek to trade the coffee duty for an income tax | **Supported; the bargain reopens in 1961** |

**The story in one paragraph.** 1960 is the end of the coffee decade and of the PRUD. The 1958–59 bust had been absorbed by drawing down the coffee-boom surplus and cutting investment, without touching the development share. In 1960 revenue recovered through import duties, the deficit nearly closed, and the budget gave education its largest share of the decade. The political crisis did not come from a fiscal collapse. It came when a regime that was expanding the University and the schools, opening elections a little and offering a rural minimum wage, faced an opposition that had grown with the Cuban revolution and an elite that resisted the reforms. In August the regime chose order: the Ley de Reuniones, expulsions, the assault on the University and the state of siege, all carried out with a security budget that did not grow. Reserves fell, the coffee growers asked to change how they were taxed, the minimum wage was never voted, and on 26 October the officers removed Lemus without a shot. The contradiction at the heart of the project, a modernizing State that builds an educated public and will not tolerate its dissent, is fully visible in 1960, and so is its fiscal signature: development and security pulling in the same budget, with the coffee cycle setting the room between them.
