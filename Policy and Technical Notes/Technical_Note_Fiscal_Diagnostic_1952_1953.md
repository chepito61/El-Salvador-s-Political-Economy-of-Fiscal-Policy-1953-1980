# El Salvador: Fiscal Diagnostic and Vulnerability Assessment, 1952–1953

**Technical Note — Preliminary Assessment Based on Primary Fiscal Data**

---

## Executive Summary

This note presents a preliminary fiscal diagnostic for El Salvador covering fiscal years 1952 and 1953, drawing exclusively on data from the Memorias de Hacienda (annual reports of the Ministry of Finance). The analysis applies standard fiscal assessment frameworks — of the kind used by the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and comparable institutions — to evaluate the structural soundness and vulnerability of El Salvador's fiscal position during this period.

**Key findings:**

- El Salvador's fiscal position in 1952–53 was **fundamentally sound but structurally fragile**. The government ran headline surpluses in both years, debt was modest and declining, and debt service was manageable.
- However, the revenue base was **dangerously concentrated** in trade taxes (87% of total revenue), with customs duties alone accounting for over 70% of all government income. This created an extreme exposure to coffee price cycles.
- **Expenditure rigidity was high**: the wage bill absorbed 44–48% of total spending, leaving limited room for fiscal adjustment in a downturn.
- **A dual-budget architecture** — separating ordinary and extraordinary budgets — meant that the headline surplus of the General Fund understated the government's true fiscal footprint. Off-budget capital commitments financed through earmarked borrowing added a layer of fiscal activity not captured in the headline numbers.
- **Debt was not a constraint but was poorly diversified**: the Empréstito Nacional de 1922 represented 62% of total public debt, creating concentration risk in a single legacy instrument.

**Currency note:** El Salvador maintained a fixed exchange rate of US$1.00 = ₡2.50 (colones) throughout the period under study. All figures are in colones unless otherwise indicated.

| Headline Balance as % of Revenue            | 17.1%           | 11.6%           |
| Superávit Financiero (end of year)          | ₡4,061,331      | ₡1,257,000 (est.) |

The General Fund recorded substantial surpluses in both years. However, the **Superávit Financiero** — the government's actual cash-and-obligations position — was considerably smaller (₡4.1 million at end-1952, declining to approximately ₡1.3 million at end-1953), reflecting the difference between budget-basis accounting and the treasury's actual liquidity position.

**Interpretation:** The wide gap between the headline surplus and the financial surplus (Superávit Financiero) signals that a significant portion of budgeted revenue was not yet collected (accrued but unpaid customs duties), and that outstanding commitments and reserves absorbed much of the nominal excess. This is a common pattern in fiscally dependent trade-tax economies: revenues are booked on an accrual basis linked to import/export policies, but cash flows are lumpy and seasonal.

## 2. Structural Revenue Analysis

### 2.1 Revenue Concentration Index

A critical vulnerability indicator is the degree to which revenue depends on a narrow base. The following decomposition quantifies this risk:

| Revenue Source                              | 1953 Share (%) | Risk Assessment         |
|:--------------------------------------------|:--------------:|:------------------------|
| Import duties                                | ~55–60%        | HIGH: procyclical, trade-dependent |
| Export duties (coffee-linked)                | ~22–25%        | VERY HIGH: single-commodity exposure |
| Direct taxes                                 | ~5–7%          | LOW: small but stable    |
| Fees, licenses, services                     | ~7–8%          | MODERATE: somewhat stable |
| Other (state enterprises, misc.)             | ~5–6%          | MODERATE                 |
| **Trade-dependent share (imports + exports)**| **~80%**       | **CRITICAL**             |

**Assessment:** With approximately 80% of revenue dependent on trade flows — and trade flows overwhelmingly driven by a single export commodity (coffee) — El Salvador's revenue base exhibited an extreme concentration risk. A 20% decline in coffee prices could, through its effects on both export duties and the import purchasing power it generated, reduce total government revenue by 12–16%.

The 1952 experience confirmed this vulnerability: customs revenue declined after the end of the 1950–51 coffee boom, despite continued growth in other revenue categories.

### 2.2 Revenue Buoyancy (Preliminary)

Revenue buoyancy measures the responsiveness of total revenue to economic growth. Using the five-year revenue series from the 1953 Memoria (in the absence of GDP data for this period):

| Period     | Revenue Growth | Implied Buoyancy Assessment |
|:-----------|:--------------:|:----------------------------|
| 1949–1950  | +21%           | Boom phase: Korean War commodity surge |
| 1950–1951  | +41%           | Peak coffee boom            |
| 1951–1952  | +8%            | Correction: post-boom normalization |
| 1952–1953  | +10%           | Moderate recovery           |

**[DATA GAP: GDP series for 1952–1953 is needed to compute formal buoyancy coefficients. The BCR's historical national accounts for 1950–1964 should provide nominal GDP in colones corrientes. Pending receipt of this data.]**

The revenue pattern is consistent with a **highly buoyant but volatile** fiscal system: revenues surge during commodity booms (1950–51) but decelerate sharply when external conditions normalize (1952). This boom-bust revenue pattern is a hallmark of trade-dependent fiscal systems and represents the single most important structural vulnerability.

This is both a vulnerability (trade dependence) and a potential fiscal space reserve (room to develop direct taxation if needed).

## 3. Expenditure Rigidity Analysis

### 3.1 Wage Bill Rigidity

| Indicator                                   | 1952            | 1953            | Alert Threshold |
|:--------------------------------------------|:---------------:|:---------------:|:---------------:|
| Personnel spending / Total expenditure       | 48.2%           | 49.7%*          | >50%            |
| Personnel spending / Total revenue           | 39.9%           | 44.0%           | >45%            |

*1953 figure combines Salarios Permanentes (43.5%) + Otros Servicios Personales (6.2%).

**Assessment:** The wage bill was approaching critical thresholds. At nearly 50% of total expenditure, personnel costs represented the single largest spending category and the most rigid component of the budget. In a revenue downturn, the government would have very limited room to cut spending without either reducing the wage bill (politically costly) or cutting investment and subsidies (developmentally damaging).

This is a concerning structural feature, particularly given the revenue volatility documented above.

### 4.3 Debt Dynamics Assessment

The debt trajectory in 1952–53 was **unambiguously favorable**:

- **The stock was declining** in absolute terms (₡30.6M → ₡28.4M), meaning amortization exceeded new borrowing.
- **The debt-to-revenue ratio was falling** (22.6% → 20.2%), meaning revenue growth was outpacing any net debt accumulation.
- **The implied interest rate (~3.6%)** was well below the revenue growth rate (~10% in 1953), satisfying the fundamental condition for debt sustainability: **r < g**.
- **Debt service was modest** at under 5% of expenditure and under 4% of revenue, well below standard alert thresholds.

### 4.4 Debt Concentration Risk

| Instrument                    | Share of Total Debt (1953) | Risk Factor                |
|:------------------------------|:--------------------------:|:---------------------------|
| Empréstito Nacional 1922      | 62.3%                      | Legacy, declining, external |
| Empréstito Aprovisionamiento  | 14.1%                      | Eximbank, amortizing        |
| Letras del Tesoro 5%          | 14.1%                      | Domestic, short-term risk   |
| Other (roads, port, fiscal)   | 9.5%                       | Project-specific            |

**Assessment:** The debt portfolio was concentrated in a single legacy instrument (the 1922 loan), which while being retired, still represented nearly two-thirds of total obligations. The Letras del Tesoro (5% Treasury Letters) at ₡4 million represent potential rollover risk, as short-term instruments require periodic renewal. However, at 14% of total debt and under 3% of annual revenue, this was manageable.

### 4.5 Fiscal Space Estimate

Given the modest debt burden, we can estimate the government's **theoretical borrowing capacity** — the additional debt it could take on before reaching standard distress thresholds:

| Scenario                                    | Current (1953)   | Threshold  | Available Space  |
|:--------------------------------------------|:----------------:|:----------:|:----------------:|
| Debt service / expenditure                   | 4.3%             | 15%        | ₡13.3 million*  |
| Debt service / revenue                       | 3.8%             | 20%        | ₡22.7 million*  |
| Debt / revenue                               | 20.2%            | 60%        | ₡55.8 million   |

*Assumes new borrowing at 5% interest with 15-year amortization.

**Interpretation:** The government had substantial fiscal space for additional borrowing — potentially doubling or tripling its debt stock before approaching conventional distress thresholds. This fiscal space would become relevant as the development program expanded in subsequent years, particularly for infrastructure projects (roads, ports, hydroelectric power) that would require external financing.

## 8. Preliminary Assessment and Outlook

### 8.1 Overall Assessment

El Salvador's fiscal position in 1952–53 can be characterized as **low debt, low stress, high vulnerability**. The government's balance sheet was strong: debt was modest and declining, service was manageable, and the budget ran surpluses. But this strength was built on a structurally fragile foundation — a revenue base overwhelmingly dependent on coffee-driven trade taxes, with minimal diversification into direct taxation or non-trade sources.

The fiscal architecture had a dual character: a disciplined General Fund that aligned spending to available resources through the quota system, and a parallel set of extraordinary budgets and earmarked funds that financed infrastructure investment through dedicated borrowing. This structure worked well in a period of rising revenues, but contained embedded risks for a downturn: the ordinary budget's rigidity (75% non-discretionary) would collide with the revenue base's volatility.

### 8.2 Forward-Looking Risks

1. **A sustained decline in coffee prices** would simultaneously reduce export duties, import duties (as reduced export earnings curtailed import capacity), and customs revenue — potentially triggering a fiscal crisis within one to two years. No stabilization fund or countercyclical mechanism was in place.

2. **The draw-down of authorized extraordinary spending** — particularly the ₡11.15 million Valle de La Esperanza authorization — would substantially increase the government's debt and annual service requirements, potentially doubling the debt stock.

3. **The retirement of the 1922 loan**, while positive for debt reduction, also freed fiscal space that would likely be filled by new borrowing for infrastructure. The question is whether new borrowing would be on as favorable terms.

4. **Wage bill momentum** — with personnel costs approaching 50% of spending — would increasingly crowd out investment and other discretionary spending, particularly if revenue growth decelerated.
